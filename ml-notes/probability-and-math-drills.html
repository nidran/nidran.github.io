Probability & Math Drills

Worked problems turn formulas into reflexes; every numeric answer below was checked by calculation or simulation.

Interview math questions rarely ask for a theorem. They ask for a number or a derivation under time pressure, and the scoring is on setup, sanity checks, and clean statements of assumptions. This chapter collects 26 practice problems with full solutions, grouped by the skill they train. Try each problem before reading the solution. Background lives in Probability & Statistics, Math, Maximum Likelihood Estimation, and Eigenvectors, PCA and SVD.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Set up a Bayes problem with natural frequencies and update sequentially with likelihood ratios.
  • Use indicator variables and linearity of expectation to avoid hard joint distributions.
  • Pick between Poisson, exponential, geometric, and binomial from the generative story, and use memorylessness correctly.
  • Derive the MLE for Bernoulli, Gaussian, and Poisson models in a few lines and state the bias of each.
  • Derive the gradient of least squares, softmax with cross-entropy, and the ridge closed form from scratch.
  • Read PCA and SVD facts off a covariance matrix, check convexity with a Hessian, and apply Jensen and tail bounds.
Memory hook

Name the model, write the likelihood or the indicator, simplify, then sanity-check against a limiting case.

#Bayes and conditional probability

#Problem 1: repeated positive tests

A condition has prevalence 0.010.010.01. A test has sensitivity 0.900.900.90 and false positive rate 0.050.050.05. Two independent tests (independent given the true state) both come back positive. What is the probability of the condition?

Solution. Use odds. Prior odds are 0.01/0.990.01/0.990.01/0.99. Each positive multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio 0.90/0.05=180.90/0.05 = 180.90/0.05=18. Posterior odds are 0.010.99⋅182=32499\frac{0.01}{0.99}\cdot 18^2 = \frac{324}{99}0.990.01​⋅182=99324​, so the probability is 32499+324≈0.766\frac{324}{99+324}\approx 0.76699+324324​≈0.766 (one positive gives about 0.1540.1540.154). Verified numerically: 0.76600.76600.7660.

Interview angle

The weak answer recomputes the full fraction for every new test. The strong answer states the independence-given-state assumption and multiplies by the likelihood ratio.

#Problem 2: two children

A family has two children. (a) Given at least one is a boy, what is the probability both are boys? (b) Given at least one is a boy born on a Tuesday?

Solution. (a) The equally likely outcomes are BB, BG, GB, GG; conditioning removes GG, leaving three outcomes of which one is BB: 1/31/31/3. (b) Enumerate (sex, weekday) pairs: 142=19614^2=196142=196 outcomes. Those with at least one Tuesday boy: 196−132=27196 - 13^2 = 27196−132=27. Of those, 72−62=137^2-6^2=1372−62=13 have two boys with at least one born on a Tuesday. Answer 13/27≈0.48113/27\approx 0.48113/27≈0.481. Simulation gave 0.33340.33340.3334 and 0.48130.48130.4813. The extra detail changes the answer because it makes "two boys" more likely to contain a Tuesday boy.

#Problem 3: Monty Hall

Three doors, one prize. A contestant picks a door, the host (who knows where the prize is) opens a different door without the prize, and offers a switch. What is the winning probability of switching?

Solution. The first pick is right with probability 1/31/31/3. If it was wrong (probability 2/32/32/3), the host's reveal leaves the prize behind the one remaining door, so switching wins. Switching wins with probability 2/32/32/3; simulation gave 0.66640.66640.6664. The result depends on the host knowing and avoiding the prize; if the host opened a door at random and it happened to be empty, the answer becomes 1/21/21/2.

P(spam)=0.3P(\text{spam})=0.3P(spam)=0.3. Word A appears in 60%60\%60% of spam and 10%10\%10% of ham. Word B appears in 50%50\%50% of spam and 20%20\%20% of ham. Assuming the words are conditionally independent given the class, what is P(spam∣A,B)P(\text{spam}\mid A,B)P(spam∣A,B)?

#Problem 8: waiting for a pattern

A fair coin is flipped repeatedly. What is the expected number of flips until (a) HH first appears, (b) HT first appears?

Solution. Use a first-step argument. For HH, let eee be the expected time from scratch and eHe_HeH​ the expected remaining time after one H. Simulation gave about 6.06.06.0 and 4.04.04.0. Both patterns are equally likely in any window of two flips, but HH overlaps with itself, which delays it.

#Problem 9: law of total variance

A binary ZZZ is 000 or 111 with equal probability. Given Z=0Z=0Z=0, X∼N(0,1)X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)X∼N(0,1); given Z=1Z=1Z=1, X∼N(4,1)X\sim\mathcal{N}(4,1)X∼N(4,1). Find Var(X)\text{Var}(X)Var(X).

Total variance =1+4=5=1+4=5=1+4=5; simulation gave 4.9964.9964.996. The same split explains why total variance in a pooled dataset exceeds the within-group variance when group means differ.

#Problem 10: variance of an average with correlation

#Common distributions

#Problem 11: Poisson arrivals

Requests arrive as a Poisson process at 3 per hour. Find (a) the probability of no request in 30 minutes, (b) the probability of at least 5 in one hour.

Solution. (a) The count in 30 minutes is Poisson with mean 1.51.51.5, so P(0)=e−1.5≈0.2231P(0)=e^{-1.5}\approx0.2231P(0)=e−1.5≈0.2231. (b) For mean 333, P(N≥5)=1−∑k=04e−33k/k!≈0.1847P(N\ge5)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{4}e^{-3}3^k/k!\approx0.1847P(N≥5)=1−∑k=04​e−33k/k!≈0.1847 (computed numerically). The count variance equals its mean, which is a check against overdispersion in real data.

#Problem 12: exponential memorylessness

A component has an exponential lifetime with mean 100 hours. Given that it has survived 100 hours, what is the probability it survives past 150 hours?

Solution. By memorylessness P(T>150∣T>100)=P(T>50)=e−50/100=e−0.5≈0.6065P(T>150\mid T>100)=P(T>50)=e^{-50/100}=e^{-0.5}\approx0.6065P(T>150∣T>100)=P(T>50)=e−50/100=e−0.5≈0.6065. Simulation gave 0.60720.60720.6072. The result holds only if the hazard rate is constant; wear-out or burn-in behavior violates it.

#Problem 13: geometric waiting

A fair die is rolled until a 6 appears. What is the expected number of rolls, and the probability that the first 6 occurs on roll 10 or later?

Solution. The count is geometric with p=1/6p=1/6p=1/6, so the mean is 1/p=61/p=61/p=6. The first 6 is on roll 10 or later exactly when the first nine rolls contain no 6, which has probability (5/6)9≈0.1938(5/6)^9\approx0.1938(5/6)9≈0.1938. Simulation gave mean 5.9965.9965.996 and tail 0.19330.19330.1933.

#Problem 15: competing exponentials

#Problem 17: Bernoulli

#Problem 21: softmax with cross-entropy

#PCA and SVD facts

#Problem 23: variance explained

A two-feature covariance matrix is (3113)\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}(31​13​). Find the principal components and the variance explained by the first.

Solution. The eigenvalues satisfy (3−λ)2=1(3-\lambda)^2=1(3−λ)2=1, giving λ=4\lambda=4λ=4 and λ=2\lambda=2λ=2. The first eigenvector is (1,1)/2(1,1)/\sqrt2(1,1)/2​. Variance explained is 4/(4+2)=2/34/(4+2)=2/34/(4+2)=2/3. Computed numerically: eigenvalues [2,4][2,4][2,4], first component (0.707,0.707)(0.707,0.707)(0.707,0.707). Sampling 10510^5105 points from this distribution and taking singular values of the centered data gave σi2/(n−1)≈[3.995,2.003]\sigma_i^2/(n-1)\approx[3.995,2.003]σi2​/(n−1)≈[3.995,2.003], matching the eigenvalues; this is the general link between singular values of centered data and covariance eigenvalues.

#Problem 24: rank after centering

Three points in five dimensions are centered at their mean. What is the maximum rank of the centered data matrix, and how many principal components can have nonzero variance?

Solution. Centering subtracts the mean row from each row, so the three rows sum to zero and are linearly dependent. The rank is at most min⁡(n−1,d)=2\min(n-1,d)=2min(n−1,d)=2 (a random example gave rank 2). At most two components carry variance. This is why PCA with fewer samples than features cannot recover more than n−1n-1n−1 directions. For the best rank-kkk approximation in Frobenius norm, the Eckart-Young theorem keeps the top kkk singular values; for diag(3,1)\text{diag}(3,1)diag(3,1) the singular values are 333 and 111, so the rank-1 error has norm 111.

#Convexity and inequalities

#Problem 25: Hessian test

Is f(x,y)=x2+xy+y2f(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2f(x,y)=x2+xy+y2 convex? Is g(x,y)=x2+4xy+y2g(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2g(x,y)=x2+4xy+y2?

Solution. A twice-differentiable function is convex when its Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere. For fff the Hessian is (2112)\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}(21​12​) with eigenvalues 111 and 333, so fff is convex (strictly). For ggg the Hessian is (2442)\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}(24​42​) with eigenvalues −2-2−2 and 666, so ggg is not convex; it is a saddle. Logistic loss in the weights is convex because its second derivative in the margin is σ(1−σ)≥0\sigma(1-\sigma)\ge0σ(1−σ)≥0 and composition with a linear map preserves convexity; log-sum-exp is also convex.

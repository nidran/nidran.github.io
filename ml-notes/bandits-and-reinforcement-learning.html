Bandits & Reinforcement Learning Basics

Learning from the consequences of your own actions. Core vocabulary, the algorithms worth knowing by heart, and how to choose between bandit, RL and supervised formulations in a product.

Supervised learning assumes labels are given. When a system chooses what to show, the data it later learns from depends on those choices, and there is a trade-off between using what is known and gathering information. Bandits and reinforcement learning formalize that setting. For the recommendation application see Recommender Systems; for the broader interview structure see ML System Design Framework.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Define an MDP, the value functions, and the Bellman equation.
  • Distinguish value-based from policy-based methods, and on-policy from off-policy.
  • Write the Q-learning update and the policy gradient estimator at a glance.
  • Implement epsilon-greedy, UCB and Thompson sampling and state their regret behavior under stated assumptions.
  • Explain LinUCB for contextual bandits.
  • Describe IPS and doubly robust off-policy evaluation, with their conditions.
  • Decide among supervised learning, a bandit, and full RL for a product problem.

#The Landscape

Setting State Action affects future states? Feedback Example
Supervised None (inputs given) No Correct label Spam classification
Multi-armed bandit None No Reward of the chosen arm only Which headline variant to show
Contextual bandit Context xxx per round No Reward of the chosen action only Choose an article for this user now
Reinforcement learning State evolves Yes Delayed reward Multi-step dialogue, inventory control, game play

Bandit feedback is partial: you see the reward only for the action taken, never for the counterfactual alternatives. RL adds sequential dependence: actions change future states and rewards.

#Markov Decision Processes

Memory hook

MDP = states, actions, transitions, reward, discount: the future depends on the present only.

An MDP is a tuple (S,A,P,r,γ)(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)(S,A,P,r,γ):

  • States S\mathcal{S}S, actions A\mathcal{A}A.
  • Transition kernel P(s′∣s,a)P(s'\mid s,a)P(s′∣s,a) satisfying the Markov property: the next state depends on the history only through the current state and action.
  • Reward r(s,a)r(s,a)r(s,a) (or r(s,a,s′)r(s,a,s')r(s,a,s′)) and discount γ∈[0,1)\gamma\in[0,1)γ∈[0,1).

A policy π(a∣s)\pi(a\mid s)π(a∣s) maps states to action distributions. The objective is to maximize expected discounted return Gt=∑k≥0γkrt+kG_t=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t+k}Gt​=∑k≥0​γkrt+k​. The discount keeps the sum finite and expresses a preference for earlier rewards; γ\gammaγ near 1 gives a long horizon, and the effective horizon is about 1/(1−γ)1/(1-\gamma)1/(1−γ).

#Value functions

Vπ(s)=Eπ[Gt∣st=s],Qπ(s,a)=Eπ[Gt∣st=s,at=a]V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t\mid s_t=s\big],\qquad Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t\mid s_t=s,a_t=a\big]Vπ(s)=Eπ​[Gt​∣st​=s],Qπ(s,a)=Eπ​[Gt​∣st​=s,at​=a]

The Bellman expectation equation expresses value recursively:

Qπ(s,a)=r(s,a)+γ Es′∼P[∑a′π(a′∣s′)Qπ(s′,a′)]Q^\pi(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P}\Big[\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')Q^\pi(s',a')\Big]Qπ(s,a)=r(s,a)+γEs′∼P​[a′∑​π(a′∣s′)Qπ(s′,a′)]

The Bellman optimality equation characterizes the best achievable value:

Q∗(s,a)=r(s,a)+γ Es′[max⁡a′Q∗(s′,a′)],π∗(s)∈arg⁡max⁡aQ∗(s,a)Q^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\Big[\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\Big],\qquad \pi^*(s)\in\arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)Q∗(s,a)=r(s,a)+γEs′​[a′max​Q∗(s′,a′)],π∗(s)∈argamax​Q∗(s,a)

The optimality operator is a γ\gammaγ-contraction in the max norm, so repeatedly applying it (value iteration) converges to Q∗Q^*Q∗ when the model is known and the state-action space is finite.

Interview angle

The one-sentence difference between VVV and QQQ: VVV scores a state under a policy; QQQ scores a state-action pair and therefore lets you pick actions without a model of the environment, which is why model-free methods learn QQQ.

Other axes to name:

  • Model-free vs model-based: whether a model of PPP and rrr is learned and used for planning.
  • On-policy vs off-policy: on-policy methods learn about the policy that generates the data; off-policy methods can learn about one policy from data generated by another, which enables reuse of logs and replay buffers.

#Q-Learning

Q-learning is an off-policy temporal-difference method. After observing (s,a,r,s′)(s,a,r,s')(s,a,r,s′):

Q(s,a)←Q(s,a)+α[r+γmax⁡a′Q(s′,a′)−Q(s,a)]Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\Big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\Big]Q(s,a)←Q(s,a)+α[r+γa′max​Q(s′,a′)−Q(s,a)]

The bracketed term is the TD error: a bootstrapped target r+γmax⁡a′Q(s′,a′)r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')r+γmaxa′​Q(s′,a′) minus the current estimate. It is off-policy because the target uses the greedy action regardless of which action the behavior policy takes next (SARSA, in contrast, uses the action actually taken and is on-policy). In the tabular case, Q-learning converges to Q∗Q^*Q∗ under standard conditions: every state-action pair is visited infinitely often and the step sizes satisfy the usual stochastic-approximation conditions.

With neural networks (DQN-style), the update becomes regression on the target y=r+γmax⁡a′Qθˉ(s′,a′)y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\bar\theta}(s',a')y=r+γmaxa′​Qθˉ​(s′,a′) with two stabilizers:

  • Experience replay: sample past transitions at random to decorrelate updates.
  • Target network: θˉ\bar\thetaθˉ is a slowly updated copy of θ\thetaθ, so the target does not chase itself.

The combination of function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy data (the "deadly triad") can diverge; these tricks reduce, but do not eliminate, the risk. Taking the max over noisy estimates also causes overestimation bias, addressed by Double Q-learning (select the action with one network, evaluate it with another).

where Ψt\Psi_tΨt​ is a signal for how good the action was. The log-derivative trick is what makes this possible: the gradient passes through the sampling distribution without differentiating the environment.

PPO limits how far each update moves the policy by clipping the probability ratio ρt=πθ(at∣st)/πθold(at∣st)\rho_t=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)ρt​=πθ​(at​∣st​)/πθold​​(at​∣st​): it maximizes E[min⁡(ρtAt, clip(ρt,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)At)]\mathbb{E}\big[\min(\rho_t A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t)\big]E[min(ρt​At​, clip(ρt​,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)At​)]. This keeps updates inside a region where the old data is still a reasonable sample for the new policy. PPO is widely used, including in preference-based fine-tuning of language models (RLHF), where the "environment" is a learned reward model.

Go deeper

Stochastic policies make exploration automatic and permit continuous actions; the cost is sample inefficiency. Value-based methods reuse data more aggressively. Many practical systems combine them (actor-critic with a replay buffer), which brings back the stability issues of off-policy learning.

#Exploration vs Exploitation

Memory hook

UCB is optimistic, Thompson sampling is random in proportion to belief, epsilon-greedy is random regardless of belief.

Exploitation picks the action that currently looks best; exploration picks others to improve estimates. A system that never explores may lock onto a suboptimal action because its estimates for the others are never corrected. Formally, performance is measured by regret: the cumulative gap between the reward of the best fixed action and the reward obtained,

Regret(T)=Tμ∗−E[∑t=1Trt]\text{Regret}(T)=T\mu^*-\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^T r_t\Big]Regret(T)=Tμ∗−E[t=1∑T​rt​]

Constant exploration gives linear regret; good algorithms achieve sublinear regret (logarithmic in TTT for stochastic bandits with the right assumptions).

#Multi-Armed Bandits

KKK arms, arm kkk has unknown mean reward μk\mu_kμk​. Each round pick an arm, observe a reward (assumed i.i.d. per arm for the stochastic bandit setting). Let Nk(t)N_k(t)Nk​(t) be pulls of arm kkk and μ^k\hat\mu_kμ^​k​ its sample mean.

#Epsilon-greedy

With probability ϵ\epsilonϵ pick a uniformly random arm; otherwise pick arg⁡max⁡kμ^k\arg\max_k\hat\mu_kargmaxk​μ^​k​.

  • Simple, easy to implement, easy to explain.
  • Wasteful: exploration is uniform, ignoring uncertainty, and a fixed ϵ\epsilonϵ yields linear regret. Decaying ϵt∝1/t\epsilon_t\propto 1/tϵt​∝1/t (with a suitable constant tied to the reward gaps) can recover logarithmic regret, but needs knowledge of those gaps to tune.

#UCB (Upper Confidence Bound)

Optimism in the face of uncertainty: pick the arm with the highest plausible mean.

at=arg⁡max⁡k[μ^k+2ln⁡tNk(t)]a_t=\arg\max_k\Big[\hat\mu_k+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{N_k(t)}}\Big]at​=argkmax​[μ^​k​+Nk​(t)2lnt​​]

(this is UCB1 for rewards in [0,1][0,1][0,1]). The bonus shrinks as an arm is pulled more, so under-sampled arms get tried, and clearly bad arms stop being pulled once their upper bound falls below the best arm's mean. UCB1 attains O(log⁡T)O(\log T)O(logT) regret for stochastic bounded rewards. It is deterministic given the history, which can be inconvenient for delayed or batched feedback (all concurrent requests pick the same arm).

#Thompson sampling

Maintain a posterior over each arm's parameter; sample one value per arm from the posterior and play the arm with the largest sample.

For Bernoulli rewards (click or no click) with a Beta(1,1)\text{Beta}(1,1)Beta(1,1) prior, after sks_ksk​ successes and fkf_kfk​ failures: θk∼Beta(1+sk, 1+fk)\theta_k\sim\text{Beta}(1+s_k,\,1+f_k)θk​∼Beta(1+sk​,1+fk​), play arg⁡max⁡kθk\arg\max_k\theta_kargmaxk​θk​.

  • Exploration is proportional to the probability an arm is best given current beliefs.
  • Randomized, so it handles delayed and batched feedback gracefully.
  • Often performs comparably to or better than UCB empirically, and has matching regret guarantees for several reward models.
  • Requires a reasonable model of the reward and prior; with a misspecified model the guarantees do not hold.
Algorithm Exploration mechanism Needs Notes
Epsilon-greedy Random with probability ϵ\epsilonϵ Only μ^\hat\muμ^​ Baseline; tuning of ϵ\epsilonϵ matters
UCB Optimism bonus ∝1/Nk\propto 1/\sqrt{N_k}∝1/Nk​​ Bounded or sub-Gaussian rewards Deterministic; strong theory

Non-stationarity. If arm means drift (trends, seasonality, content freshness), use discounted or sliding-window estimates, or a floor on exploration, otherwise old data keeps estimates stale.

Interview angle

Asked "A/B test vs bandit?" Answer with the trade-off. A/B tests give clean, unbiased inference about effect sizes and long-horizon metrics for a fixed allocation. Bandits shift traffic toward better arms during the experiment (lower regret) but complicate statistical inference, are sensitive to non-stationarity and delayed rewards, and optimize one reward metric, so they fit short-lived, low-stakes, many-variant optimization such as creatives or ranking blends rather than long-term guardrail-heavy decisions.

#Contextual Bandits

In each round a context xtx_txt​ is observed (user, time, device), the learner picks an action ata_tat​, and sees reward rtr_trt​ for that action only. The goal is a policy π(a∣x)\pi(a\mid x)π(a∣x) with high expected reward. The difference from RL is that actions do not influence future contexts; the difference from supervised learning is partial feedback.

#LinUCB

Assume expected reward is linear in features: E[r∣x,a]=θa⊤x\mathbb{E}[r\mid x,a]=\theta_a^\top xE[r∣x,a]=θa⊤​x (the disjoint model; a shared-parameter version uses joint features ϕ(x,a)\phi(x,a)ϕ(x,a)). Per action keep

Aa=λI+∑t:at=axtxt⊤,ba=∑t:at=artxt,θ^a=Aa−1baA_a=\lambda I+\sum_{t:a_t=a}x_tx_t^\top,\qquad b_a=\sum_{t:a_t=a}r_tx_t,\qquad \hat\theta_a=A_a^{-1}b_aAa​=λI+t:at​=a∑​xt​xt⊤​,ba​=t:at​=a∑​rt​xt​,θ^a​=Aa−1​ba​

(a ridge-regression estimate) and choose

at=arg⁡max⁡a θ^a⊤xt+αxt⊤Aa−1xta_t=\arg\max_a\ \hat\theta_a^\top x_t+\alpha\sqrt{x_t^\top A_a^{-1}x_t}at​=argamax​ θ^a⊤​xt​+αxt⊤​Aa−1​xt​​

The second term is the width of a confidence interval for the prediction: large when the context xtx_txt​ lies in directions where action aaa has little data. α\alphaα controls exploration. Linear Thompson sampling is the randomized counterpart: sample θ~a∼N(θ^a, σ2Aa−1)\tilde\theta_a\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat\theta_a,\ \sigma^2A_a^{-1})θ~a​∼N(θ^a​, σ2Aa−1​) and act greedily on the sample.

Considerations:

  • The regret guarantees need the linear model to hold (or be approximately right). With neural reward models, practical variants use ensembles, dropout or last-layer Bayesian linear regression for uncertainty.
  • Features should be normalized; AaA_aAa​ updates are rank-one, so the inverse can be maintained incrementally.
  • Logging the probability of the chosen action is essential for later off-policy evaluation.

#Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE) at a Glance

Memory hook

IPS reweights, DM models, DR does both and needs only one to be right.

Goal: estimate the value of a new (target) policy π\piπ from logs collected by a different logging policy μ\muμ, without deploying it. Logged data: (xi,ai,ri,μ(ai∣xi))(x_i,a_i,r_i,\mu(a_i\mid x_i))(xi​,ai​,ri​,μ(ai​∣xi​)).

Direct method (DM): fit a reward model r^(x,a)\hat r(x,a)r^(x,a) and compute V^DM=1n∑i∑aπ(a∣xi)r^(xi,a)\hat V_{DM}=\frac1n\sum_i\sum_a\pi(a\mid x_i)\hat r(x_i,a)V^DM​=n1​∑i​∑a​π(a∣xi​)r^(xi​,a). Low variance; biased if the reward model is wrong.

Inverse propensity scoring (IPS): reweight logged rewards by how much more (or less) likely the target would have taken the logged action:

V^IPS=1n∑i=1nπ(ai∣xi)μ(ai∣xi) ri\hat V_{IPS}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}\,r_iV^IPS​=n1​i=1∑n​μ(ai​∣xi​)π(ai​∣xi​)​ri​

Unbiased if (1) the logging propensities μ\muμ are known (or correctly estimated) and (2) overlap holds: μ(a∣x)>0\mu(a\mid x)>0μ(a∣x)>0 wherever π(a∣x)>0\pi(a\mid x)>0π(a∣x)>0. Variance can be large when weights are large, so clipping or self-normalization is used (introducing some bias, in exchange for lower variance).

Doubly robust (DR): combine both,

V^DR=1n∑i[∑aπ(a∣xi)r^(xi,a)+π(ai∣xi)μ(ai∣xi)(ri−r^(xi,ai))]\hat V_{DR}=\frac1n\sum_i\Big[\sum_a\pi(a\mid x_i)\hat r(x_i,a)+\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}\big(r_i-\hat r(x_i,a_i)\big)\Big]V^DR​=n1​i∑​[a∑​π(a∣xi​)r^(xi​,a)+μ(ai​∣xi​)π(ai​∣xi​)​(ri​−r^(xi​,ai​))]

It is consistent if either the reward model or the propensities are correct (hence "doubly robust"), and typically has lower variance than IPS when r^\hat rr^ is reasonable.

Estimator Bias Variance Requires
DM Depends on reward-model error Low Good reward model

DR Unbiased if propensities or model correct Medium Both available

For sequences (full RL) the importance weights multiply over time steps, so variance can grow exponentially with horizon, which is why trajectory-level OPE is much harder than bandit OPE; per-decision weights, fitted-Q evaluation, and DR variants for RL are used.

Practical conditions

OPE fails silently when the logging policy never took the actions the new policy prefers (no overlap), when propensities were not logged, or when the environment changes between logging and deployment. Treat OPE as a filter that selects candidates for an online test, not a replacement for it.

#Choosing: Supervised vs Bandit vs RL in Products

Question If yes, lean toward
Are correct labels available, independent of what the system does? Supervised learning
Does the system choose an action, and you only observe the outcome of the chosen action? Contextual bandit (or supervised with IPS-weighted logs)
Do current actions meaningfully change future states or future user behavior, and is long-term reward the target? RL (or a bandit with a carefully chosen proxy reward)
Can you simulate or safely experiment at scale? RL becomes feasible; otherwise prefer offline or conservative methods

Considerations:

  • Most recommendation and ranking systems begin as supervised models trained on logged engagement, plus a small amount of exploration. Bandits layer on where freshness and uncertainty matter (new items, creative or layout selection, notification timing).
  • RL fits when long-term effects dominate, for example optimizing session-level or retention outcomes, notification pacing, or sequential decision problems such as bidding with budgets. The costs: reward design (a wrong reward is optimized relentlessly), delayed and noisy credit assignment, off-policy evaluation difficulty, instability, and the engineering burden of simulators or safe exploration.
  • A pragmatic intermediate step is to train a supervised model on proxies for long-term outcomes (for example predicted next-session return) and use it as a feature or objective term, before moving to full RL.
  • Offline RL / batch RL learns from logged data without exploration, but suffers distribution shift: the learned policy may choose actions the logs never covered, and Q estimates for those are unreliable. Conservative methods penalize such actions.
  • Guardrails apply to any learner that acts: constraints on harmful actions, fallback policies, gradual rollout, and monitoring of reward-hacking symptoms (the proxy rises while true satisfaction does not).
Interview angle

A strong framing: start from the simplest formulation that matches the data-generating process. If the decision is independent per request, it is a contextual bandit at most; escalate to RL only with a concrete argument that actions change future states in a way that matters, and say how reward, evaluation, and safe rollout will be handled.

Rapid fire: bandits and RL
  • Bandit feedback is partial: only the chosen action's reward is seen.
  • RL adds sequential dependence: actions change future states.
  • QQQ scores state-action pairs, so greedy action choice needs no model.
  • Q-learning is off-policy because its target uses the greedy next action.
  • A fixed epsilon gives linear regret; UCB and Thompson reach logarithmic regret under assumptions.
  • Log the probability of every chosen action, since IPS needs it later.
  • OPE needs overlap; treat it as a filter ahead of an online test.
  • Start from the simplest formulation: supervised, then bandit, then RL.

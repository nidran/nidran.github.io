LLM Training, Fine-tuning & RAG

LLMs: how a language model is built, adapted, decoded, grounded in external data, and served.

A deployed LLM system is the product of several stages: pretraining gives broad capability, post-training (SFT, preference optimization) shapes behavior, parameter-efficient methods make adaptation affordable, decoding turns probabilities into text, retrieval supplies knowledge the weights lack, and inference engineering determines cost and latency. Interview questions usually ask which lever to pull for a given problem, so this chapter ends with a decision table.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • State the pretraining objective and what perplexity measures, and summarize scaling laws with appropriate hedges.
  • Describe SFT, RLHF and DPO, with the key equations, and what each one fixes.
  • Explain LoRA and QLoRA with parameter and memory arithmetic.
  • Compare decoding strategies and the effect of temperature, top-kkk and top-ppp.
  • Lay out a RAG pipeline and how to evaluate each stage, and choose between prompting, RAG and fine-tuning.
  • Reason about hallucination, context windows, and inference cost.

#The objective

A decoder-only LLM is trained by next-token prediction: maximize the likelihood of the training text under the chain rule,

pθ(x1:T)=∏t=1Tpθ(xt∣x<t),L(θ)=−1T∑t=1Tlog⁡pθ(xt∣x<t).p_\theta(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}),\qquad \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}).pθ​(x1:T​)=t=1∏T​pθ​(xt​∣x<t​),L(θ)=−T1​t=1∑T​logpθ​(xt​∣x<t​).

This is cross-entropy against the one-hot "true next token," and it is self-supervised: the labels come from the text itself (see Fundamentals). Perplexity is exp⁡(L)\exp(\mathcal{L})exp(L) with L\mathcal{L}L in nats per token: roughly the effective number of equally likely choices the model is hesitating between. It depends on the tokenizer, so perplexities are comparable only between models sharing a tokenizer and evaluation text. Low perplexity indicates good fit to the text distribution; it does not by itself measure truthfulness or helpfulness (see LLM Eval).

Encoder models like BERT use masked language modeling (predict masked tokens from both sides) instead. Encoder-decoder models like T5 use span corruption. The architecture-to-objective mapping is covered in Transformers & Attention.

#What the model learns, and the data

Predicting the next token on a broad corpus forces the model to represent syntax, facts, and some reasoning patterns, because those help prediction. Data quality, deduplication, filtering, and mixture composition (web, code, books, and so on) strongly influence results, and published work suggests that data curation can matter as much as raw scale. Memorization of repeated training sequences is a known risk, with privacy and benchmark-contamination implications.

#Scaling laws (hedged)

Empirical studies report that test loss falls as a smooth power law in model size NNN, data DDD, and compute CCC, over many orders of magnitude, when the other factors are not bottlenecks:

L(N,D)≈E+ANα+BDβL(N,D) \approx E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}L(N,D)≈E+NαA​+DβB​

where EEE is an irreducible term and A,B,α,βA,B,\alpha,\betaA,B,α,β are fitted constants. Two useful rules of thumb:

  • Training compute for a dense transformer is approximately C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND FLOPs (forward plus backward, about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token forward and about twice that backward).
  • Compute-optimal allocation: early work suggested growing parameters faster than data; later work (the "Chinchilla" analysis) found that, for a fixed compute budget in its experimental setup, parameters and tokens should be scaled in roughly equal proportion, giving on the order of 20 training tokens per parameter.
Caveats on scaling laws

These are fitted trends, not laws of nature. The exponents and the "20 tokens per parameter" figure depend on architecture, data quality, tokenizer, and fitting methodology. Compute-optimal for training is not optimal for deployment: when a model will serve many requests, teams often train a smaller model on far more tokens than the compute-optimal point, because inference cost scales with NNN. Loss predictability does not guarantee that specific downstream abilities appear smoothly; whether some capabilities emerge abruptly or only appear so because of the metric used is debated.

Interview angle

Give the formula C≈6NDC\approx 6NDC≈6ND, the qualitative claim (smooth power-law improvement in loss), and the practical corollary (over-train small models when inference dominates). Flag the numbers as empirical and setup-dependent.

#Post-training: making a base model useful

A pretrained base model continues text; it does not reliably follow instructions. Post-training turns it into an assistant.

#Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

Train on (prompt, desired response) pairs with the same next-token loss, usually masking the loss on prompt tokens so only response tokens are learned. SFT teaches format and instruction-following. Evidence from several studies suggests that a relatively small set of high-quality, diverse examples can go a long way, since much of the knowledge is already in the base model; SFT mainly elicits and shapes it. Weaknesses: it imitates demonstrations rather than optimizing a preference, and it can teach a model to assert facts it does not actually know, which can encourage hallucination.

#RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), conceptually

  1. Collect preferences: for a prompt xxx, humans (or models) compare two responses and mark a preferred ywy_wyw​ and a rejected yly_lyl​.
  2. Train a reward model rϕ(x,y)r_\phi(x,y)rϕ​(x,y) on these pairs, typically with the Bradley-Terry model, where P(yw≻yl)=σ(r(x,yw)−r(x,yl))P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big)P(yw​≻yl​)=σ(r(x,yw​)−r(x,yl​)):

LRM=−E[log⁡σ(rϕ(x,yw)−rϕ(x,yl))]\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]LRM​=−E[logσ(rϕ​(x,yw​)−rϕ​(x,yl​))]

  1. Optimize the policy with RL (commonly PPO) to maximize reward while staying close to the SFT model:

max⁡πθ Ex, y∼πθ[rϕ(x,y)]−β KL(πθ(⋅∣x) ∥ πref(⋅∣x))\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\big)πθ​max​ Ex,y∼πθ​​[rϕ​(x,y)]−βKL(πθ​(⋅∣x)∥πref​(⋅∣x))

The KL term is crucial: the reward model is an imperfect proxy, and unconstrained optimization tends to find outputs that score high without being good (reward hacking, a form of Goodhart's law), such as verbose or sycophantic answers.

Costs: RLHF needs several models in memory (policy, reference, reward model, and often a value model), is sensitive to hyperparameters, and relies on preference data whose quality and annotator biases shape the result.

#DPO (direct preference optimization)

DPO observes that the KL-regularized objective above has a closed-form optimal policy, so the reward can be re-expressed through the policy itself: r(x,y)=βlog⁡πθ(y∣x)πref(y∣x)+const(x)r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y\mid x)} + \text{const}(x)r(x,y)=βlogπref​(y∣x)πθ​(y∣x)​+const(x). Substituting into the Bradley-Terry loss gives a classification-style loss on preference pairs with no separate reward model and no RL loop:

LDPO=−E[log⁡σ ⁣(βlog⁡πθ(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−βlog⁡πθ(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x))]\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]LDPO​=−E[logσ(βlogπref​(yw​∣x)πθ​(yw​∣x)​−βlogπref​(yl​∣x)πθ​(yl​∣x)​)]

It raises the relative likelihood of preferred responses over rejected ones, measured relative to the reference model.

Which of RLHF or DPO gives better final quality is task- and setup-dependent; reported comparisons are mixed, and the choice is often made on engineering simplicity. Variants exist (for example, methods with verifiable rewards for math and code, where correctness can be checked automatically, and AI-generated feedback in place of human labels).

Solving for rrr and plugging into the Bradley-Terry likelihood cancels the intractable partition function, because only reward differences between two responses to the same prompt appear.

#Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Full fine-tuning updates all NNN weights. With a common mixed-precision Adam setup, memory per parameter is roughly 16 bytes (16-bit weights 2, gradients 2, 32-bit master weights 4, two Adam moments 8), so a 7B-parameter model needs on the order of 110 GB before activations. The exact figure depends on the optimizer and precision recipe.

#LoRA (low-rank adaptation)

Freeze the pretrained weight W0∈Rd×kW_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}W0​∈Rd×k and learn a low-rank update:

W=W0+αrBA,B∈Rd×r, A∈Rr×k, r≪min⁡(d,k).W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA,\qquad B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\ r\ll\min(d,k).W=W0​+rα​BA,B∈Rd×r, A∈Rr×k, r≪min(d,k).

BBB is initialized to zero (so training starts exactly at the pretrained model) and AAA to small random values. Only AAA and BBB are trained. The hypothesis is that the weight change needed for adaptation has low intrinsic rank.

  • Parameters: r(d+k)r(d+k)r(d+k) instead of dkdkdk. For d=k=4096d=k=4096d=k=4096 and r=8r=8r=8: 8×8192=65,5368\times8192 = 65{,}5368×8192=65,536 versus about 16.816.816.8 million, around 0.4%0.4\%0.4% of that matrix.
  • Memory: gradients and optimizer states exist only for the adapters; the frozen base is stored once.
  • Serving: the update can be merged into W0W_0W0​ after training, adding no inference latency, or adapters can be kept separate and swapped per task or customer.
  • Practice: LoRA is usually applied to attention projections, often also to the FFN matrices; rank rrr and α\alphaα are hyperparameters. LoRA matches full fine-tuning on many tasks, but there are reports of a gap on tasks requiring large behavioral change or substantial new knowledge, and it can forget less (and learn less) than full fine-tuning.

#QLoRA

QLoRA combines LoRA with a 4-bit quantized frozen base model (the NF4 data type, designed for normally distributed weights), double quantization of the quantization constants, and paged optimizers to absorb memory spikes. Gradients flow through the dequantized base weights into higher-precision adapters. The base weights cost about 0.5 byte per parameter, so a 7B model's weights fit in roughly 4 GB, allowing fine-tuning of large models on a single GPU. Reported quality is close to 16-bit LoRA on the evaluated benchmarks, at the price of slower steps because of dequantization.

Interview angle

Show the arithmetic: r(d+k)r(d+k)r(d+k) trainable parameters per matrix, B=0B=0B=0 at init, mergeable at inference. Then the QLoRA add-on: quantize only the frozen base; train adapters in higher precision. Mention that "LoRA = full fine-tuning quality" is a frequent empirical outcome, not a guarantee.

#Decoding

At each step the model outputs logits zzz; a decoding strategy chooses the next token from p=softmax(z/T)p = \text{softmax}(z/T)p=softmax(z/T).

Strategy Rule Behavior
Greedy Take arg⁡max⁡\arg\maxargmax Deterministic; can be repetitive or locally optimal but globally poor
Beam search Keep the bbb highest-scoring partial sequences Finds higher-likelihood sequences; useful for translation and summarization; in open-ended generation tends to give bland, repetitive text
Temperature sampling Sample from pi∝ezi/Tp_i\propto e^{z_i/T}pi​∝ezi​/T T→0T\to0T→0 approaches greedy; T=1T=1T=1 is the model's distribution; T>1T>1T>1 flattens it
Top-kkk Sample from the kkk most probable tokens (renormalized) Cuts the unreliable tail; fixed kkk ignores how peaked the distribution is
Top-ppp (nucleus) Sample from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability ≥p\ge p≥p Adapts set size: small when the model is confident, larger when uncertain

Temperature divides logits before the softmax, so it rescales the distribution without changing token ranking. Greedy decoding is not a fixed property of "temperature 0" in all systems: floating-point nondeterminism and batching effects can still change outputs between runs. The common combination is temperature plus top-ppp (and sometimes top-kkk) applied together. Related controls include repetition or frequency penalties, stop sequences, and constrained decoding (masking tokens that violate a grammar or JSON schema, so output is valid by construction).

Interview angle

Pick the strategy by task: factual or structured output, low temperature or greedy; creative generation, moderate temperature with top-ppp. Note that sampling gives diversity but raises the chance of low-probability errors, and that lower temperature reduces but does not eliminate hallucination.

#Context window

The context window is the maximum number of tokens (prompt plus generated output) the model processes at once. It is set by the positional scheme and the length used in training or extension; longer windows raise attention cost and KV-cache memory (see Transformers & Attention).

  • A long window is not perfect recall. Several studies find that accuracy can degrade for information placed in the middle of a long prompt compared with the beginning or end ("lost in the middle"); the effect varies by model and has been reduced in newer models.
  • Cost scales with tokens: prefill time and API cost grow with prompt length; the KV cache grows linearly with it.
  • Long context and RAG are complements. Stuffing everything into the prompt is simple but costly and noisy; retrieval selects what is relevant. Which wins depends on corpus size, query pattern, and budget.

#Hallucination

Hallucination is fluent output that is unsupported by the input or by facts. Useful distinctions:

Type Meaning Typical mitigation
Intrinsic (unfaithful) Contradicts the provided source or prompt Better prompting, grounding, constrained output, faithfulness checks
Extrinsic (factual) Asserts something not verifiable or false relative to the world Retrieval, tool use, calibrated abstention

Why it happens: the training objective rewards plausible continuations, not verified truth; the model has no built-in distinction between what it knows and what is merely likely; rare facts are poorly represented; SFT on answers the model could not have known can teach confident guessing; and decoding randomness can sample unlikely tokens.

Mitigations, none of which eliminates the problem: retrieval grounding with citations, instructing the model to abstain when the context lacks the answer, lower temperature, verification passes (self-consistency, a second model or rule-based check), tool use for calculations and lookups, and fine-tuning or preference training that rewards calibrated "I don't know." Measure it with groundedness and faithfulness evaluations (see LLM Eval).

#Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

RAG places retrieved documents in the prompt so that the model answers from them. It addresses stale knowledge (the weights are frozen at a training cutoff), private or domain data, and the need for citations, without retraining.

#Pipeline

Offline (indexing): load documents →\to→ clean and parse →\to→ chunk →\to→ embed each chunk →\to→ store in an index (vector database or search engine) with metadata.

Online (query): (optional query rewriting) →\to→ embed the query →\to→ retrieve top candidates →\to→ (optional) rerank →\to→ assemble a prompt with the best chunks →\to→ generate an answer, ideally with citations.

Stage Key decisions Common failure
Chunking Size (often a few hundred tokens), overlap, respect structure (headings, paragraphs, code) Chunks too small lose context; too large dilute the embedding and waste context budget; splits that cut an answer in half
Embedding Model choice, dimension, domain fit, query vs document prefixes Off-domain embeddings miss jargon; changing the model requires re-indexing
Retrieval Dense (embedding similarity), sparse (BM25 keyword), or hybrid; kkk; metadata filters Dense retrieval can miss exact identifiers and rare terms; sparse misses paraphrases
Reranking A cross-encoder scores each (query, chunk) pair jointly, applied to the top 20 to 100 candidates Adds latency; limited by whether the right chunk was retrieved at all
Generation Prompt template, instructions to use only the context and to abstain, citation format Model ignores context, blends in parametric memory, or is distracted by irrelevant chunks

Why rerank: a bi-encoder embeds query and document independently, so it is fast (precomputed document vectors, approximate nearest-neighbor search) but coarse. A cross-encoder reads the pair together with full attention, so it is more accurate but too slow to run over the whole corpus. The standard design is a cheap high-recall first stage followed by an accurate, slower second stage on a short list.

Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) indexes such as HNSW (graph-based) or IVF (clustering-based, often with product quantization) trade a small amount of recall for large speedups over exact search.

#Evaluating RAG

Evaluate retrieval and generation separately, otherwise a bad answer cannot be diagnosed.

Layer Question Metrics
Retrieval Did the right evidence come back? Recall@kkk, precision@kkk, MRR, NDCG on a labeled query-to-relevant-chunk set
Context use Are retrieved chunks relevant and sufficient? Context relevance, context recall
Generation Is the answer supported by the context, and does it answer the question? Faithfulness (groundedness), answer relevance, correctness vs a reference
End to end Does the system meet the use case? Task success, human preference, latency, cost

Retrieval metrics are the classic ranking metrics (see Precision & Recall). LLM-as-judge methods are common for faithfulness, but the judge itself needs validation against human labels (see LLM Eval). Build a small, representative golden set of questions early, including unanswerable ones to test abstention.

RAG does not remove hallucination

If retrieval misses, the model may still answer from memory or guess. If the context is contradictory or contains instructions, the model may follow them (prompt injection through retrieved content). Treat retrieved text as untrusted input.

Go deeper (optional)

Common upgrades: query rewriting or decomposition for multi-part questions, hybrid retrieval with score fusion (for example reciprocal rank fusion), contextual or hierarchical chunking, iterative or agentic retrieval where the model decides what to look up, and graph-structured indexes for multi-hop questions. Each adds complexity, so add them in response to measured failures.

#Prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning

These are different levers, and they can be combined.

Prompt engineering RAG Fine-tuning (SFT / LoRA)
Changes The instructions and examples in the input The knowledge available at query time The model's weights
Best for First attempt on any task; format, tone, few-shot patterns Fresh, private, or large knowledge; citations; data that changes Consistent style or format, specialized behavior or skill, shorter prompts, smaller models mimicking larger ones
Weak at Knowledge the model lacks; very long instructions that are brittle Reasoning style, output behavior; quality bounded by retrieval Injecting facts reliably; keeping up with changing data
Setup cost Minimal Moderate (index, pipeline, evals) Higher (data, training, evals, redeploy)
Update cost Edit text Update the index Retrain
Per-request cost Prompt tokens Extra retrieval plus longer prompt Often lower (shorter prompts) but needs hosting
Typical risk Prompt drift, injection Retrieval misses, stale or poisoned data Overfitting, forgetting, data-quality dependence

A practical sequence, with exceptions depending on constraints: start with prompting and an eval set; add RAG if failures are due to missing or changing knowledge; add fine-tuning if failures are due to behavior, format, latency or cost that prompting cannot fix. A useful heuristic: RAG is for what the model should know, fine-tuning is for how the model should behave. The heuristic is imperfect, since fine-tuning does transfer some knowledge and retrieval can influence style.

Interview angle

Frame the answer as diagnosis: "What does the failure look like?" Wrong or missing facts point to retrieval; inconsistent format or tone point to fine-tuning or better prompting; high latency or cost with acceptable quality point to distillation, a smaller model, or caching. Say that the first step is usually an eval set, so each change is measured.

#Inference cost and latency

Serving has two phases with different bottlenecks:

Phase What happens Bound by
Prefill Process the whole prompt in parallel and fill the KV cache Compute (large matrix multiplies)
Decode Generate one token per step, reading all weights and the KV cache Memory bandwidth, typically, at small batch sizes

Back-of-envelope. A 7B model in 16-bit weights is about 14 GB. If each decode step reads all weights once, on hardware with about 1 TB/s of memory bandwidth the per-token time is bounded below by about 14 GB/1 TB/s=1414\ \text{GB} / 1\ \text{TB/s} = 1414 GB/1 TB/s=14 ms, around 70 tokens per second for a single stream, ignoring the KV cache and overheads. Batching shares the weight reads across requests, which raises throughput with little per-request latency increase until compute or cache memory becomes limiting.

Key latency metrics: time to first token (TTFT), dominated by prefill and queueing, and time per output token (inter-token latency), dominated by decode. Total cost scales with input plus output tokens.

Technique What it does Trade-off
KV cache Avoids recomputing past keys/values Memory per sequence
Continuous batching Adds and removes requests from the running batch each step Scheduler complexity
Paged KV cache Manages cache in blocks to cut fragmentation Implementation complexity
Prefix caching Reuses the cache for a shared prompt prefix Helps only when prefixes repeat
Quantization (8-bit, 4-bit weights; sometimes KV) Fewer bytes to move, smaller footprint Possible quality loss, more at lower bit widths
Speculative decoding A small draft model proposes several tokens; the large model verifies them in one pass Output distribution preserved under the standard acceptance rule; speedup depends on draft acceptance rate
GQA / MQA Smaller KV cache Architectural choice made at training time
Distillation / smaller model Cheaper per token Capability gap
Output limits, caching of repeated queries, routing easy queries to small models Cut tokens and calls Engineering and quality monitoring
Interview angle

Say "prefill is compute-bound, decode is memory-bound" and then pick optimizations accordingly: batching and quantization help decode throughput; shorter prompts, prefix caching and chunked prefill help TTFT. Tie each to a metric and a quality risk.

#Common pitfalls

  • Treating RAG as a fix for behavior problems or fine-tuning as a reliable way to add facts.
  • Comparing perplexity across different tokenizers.
  • Evaluating RAG only end to end, which hides whether retrieval or generation failed.
  • Forgetting the KL term (or β\betaβ) when explaining why RLHF and DPO do not drift arbitrarily.
  • Equating temperature 0 with guaranteed determinism or correctness.
  • Assuming larger chunks or more retrieved chunks necessarily help; extra context can dilute and distract.
Rapid fire

Pretrain on next-token cross-entropy (self-supervised); C≈6NDC\approx6NDC≈6ND; loss follows empirical power laws, and compute-optimal training is not deployment-optimal. SFT imitates demonstrations (loss on response tokens). RLHF: reward model from pairwise preferences, then KL-regularized RL. DPO: same objective, directly on preference pairs, no reward model. LoRA: W0+αrBAW_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAW0​+rα​BA, B=0B=0B=0 at init; QLoRA: 4-bit frozen base plus LoRA adapters. Decoding: greedy, beam, temperature, top-kkk, top-ppp. RAG: chunk, embed, retrieve (hybrid), rerank, generate; evaluate retrieval and generation separately. Prefill compute-bound, decode memory-bound.

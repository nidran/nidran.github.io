Computer Vision Beyond CNN Basics

From "classify one image" to "find every object, label every pixel, and learn from images without labels" — the architectures and losses an interviewer expects after the CNN basics.

The CNN chapter covers filters, shapes, and the classification head. Real vision systems add four things on top: deeper backbones that still train (ResNets), tasks with structured outputs (detection and segmentation), a different backbone family (Vision Transformers), and pretraining that needs few or no labels (contrastive learning, CLIP). Each comes with one loss or metric that interviewers like to probe: IoU and mAP, focal loss, Dice, InfoNCE.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Explain why plain deep CNNs degrade and how a skip connection fixes it, and compute the parameter saving of a bottleneck block.
  • Contrast two-stage and one-stage detectors, anchors and anchor-free heads, and say what IoU, NMS, and mAP compute, with a worked example.
  • Distinguish semantic, instance, and panoptic segmentation, and say why U-Net uses skip connections and why Dice loss helps with small foregrounds.
  • Describe how a ViT turns an image into tokens, and give a hedged answer to "ViT or CNN?"
  • Write the InfoNCE loss, and explain how CLIP does zero-shot classification.
  • Pick a transfer-learning recipe and an augmentation policy for a given data size.

#ResNets and skip connections

#The degradation problem

Stacking more layers should not hurt in principle: a deeper network could copy a shallower one and set the extra layers to identity. In practice, plain (non-residual) networks beyond a few dozen layers showed higher training error than shallower ones. This is called degradation. It is not overfitting, since the training error itself rises. It is an optimization problem: learning an identity mapping with a stack of nonlinear layers is hard for SGD, and gradients through many layers are poorly conditioned (see Vanishing & Exploding Gradients; normalization layers fixed much of the raw vanishing problem, but degradation persisted).

#The fix: learn the residual

A residual block computes

y=F(x)+x\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}y=F(x)+x

where F\mathcal{F}F is a small stack (conv, norm, ReLU, conv, norm) and x\mathbf{x}x is carried around it by an identity skip connection. If the best thing a block can do is nothing, it only needs to push F→0\mathcal{F}\to 0F→0, which is easy (weight decay and small initializations push that way). The block learns a correction to its input rather than a whole new representation.

The gradient view is the sharper argument. Differentiating y=F(x)+x\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}y=F(x)+x:

∂y∂x=∂F∂x+I\frac{\partial \mathbf{y}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} + I∂x∂y​=∂x∂F​+I

Across LLL blocks, the product of Jacobians expands into a sum of paths, and one of those paths is pure identity from the loss straight to early layers. The gradient therefore has a route that does not pass through any weight matrix or nonlinearity, which greatly reduces (though does not eliminate) vanishing gradients as depth grows. A useful reading is that a ResNet behaves like an ensemble of many paths of different depths.

Memory hook

A plain layer must rewrite its input; a residual layer only has to edit it. The "+ x" is a gradient highway.

#Shape rules and projection shortcuts

The sum requires F(x)\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})F(x) and x\mathbf{x}x to have the same shape. When a stage downsamples (stride 2) or changes channel count, the shortcut uses a 1×11\times11×1 conv (with the same stride) to match, called a projection shortcut. Everywhere else it is a parameter-free identity.

#Basic block vs bottleneck block

ResNet-18/34 use the basic block: two 3×33\times33×3 convs. Deeper variants (50/101/152) use the bottleneck block: a 1×11\times11×1 conv that squeezes channels, a 3×33\times33×3 conv at the narrow width, and a 1×11\times11×1 conv that expands back. The expensive 3×33\times33×3 operates on a quarter of the channels.

Interview angle

Weak answer: "skip connections help gradients." Strong answer: degradation is a training-error problem, not overfitting; the block reparameterizes the target as a residual so identity is the easy default; and the +I+I+I term in the Jacobian gives gradients a path that does not depend on depth. Bonus: DenseNets concatenate rather than add, and Transformers use the same residual structure around attention and MLP blocks (see Transformers & Attention).

Rapid fire — ResNets
  • Degradation: deeper plain nets have higher training error; it is optimization, not overfitting.
  • y=F(x)+x\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{x}y=F(x)+x; the Jacobian contains an identity term.
  • Shape mismatch is handled by a 1×11\times11×1 projection shortcut.
  • Bottleneck = 1×11{\times}11×1 squeeze, 3×33{\times}33×3, 1×11{\times}11×1 expand; far fewer weights than two 3×33\times33×3 at full width.
  • Batch norm follows each conv; global average pooling plus a linear layer is the head.
  • Residual connections reappear in nearly every modern architecture, including Transformers.

#Efficiency ideas at a glance

#Depthwise separable convolutions

A standard K×KK\times KK×K conv mixes space and channels in one step. A depthwise separable conv splits it: a depthwise conv applies one K×KK\times KK×K filter per input channel (spatial mixing only), then a pointwise 1×11\times11×1 conv mixes channels.

K2 Cin Cout⏟standardvsK2 Cin+Cin Cout⏟depthwise separable\underbrace{K^2\, C_{in}\, C_{out}}_{\text{standard}} \quad\text{vs}\quad \underbrace{K^2\, C_{in} + C_{in}\, C_{out}}_{\text{depthwise separable}}standardK2Cin​Cout​​​vsdepthwise separableK2Cin​+Cin​Cout​​​

For K=3K=3K=3 and Cin=Cout=128C_{in}=C_{out}=128Cin​=Cout​=128: standard =147,456=147{,}456=147,456 weights, separable =1,152+16,384=17,536=1{,}152 + 16{,}384 = 17{,}536=1,152+16,384=17,536, about 8.4 times fewer. The ratio approaches K2K^2K2 for wide layers. MobileNet-style networks are built from these, usually with an inverted bottleneck (expand, depthwise, project). The trade-off: fewer FLOPs does not always mean lower latency, because depthwise convs have low arithmetic intensity and can be memory-bound on accelerators.

#Compound scaling (EfficientNet)

A network can be scaled in depth (layers), width (channels), and input resolution. Scaling only one saturates quickly. Compound scaling grows all three together with a single coefficient ϕ\phiϕ:

depth∝αϕ,width∝βϕ,resolution∝γϕ,αβ2γ2≈2\text{depth}\propto \alpha^{\phi},\quad \text{width}\propto \beta^{\phi},\quad \text{resolution}\propto \gamma^{\phi},\qquad \alpha\beta^2\gamma^2\approx 2depth∝αϕ,width∝βϕ,resolution∝γϕ,αβ2γ2≈2

The constraint exists because FLOPs scale roughly linearly with depth and quadratically with width and resolution, so each increment of ϕ\phiϕ about doubles the compute. The constants are found by a small grid search on a base network, then ϕ\phiϕ is turned up to get the B0 to B7 family. The takeaway for interviews: scale dimensions jointly, and when tuning a backbone for a fixed budget, resolution matters as much as depth.

Rapid fire — efficiency
  • Depthwise separable: spatial and channel mixing done separately; about K2K^2K2 times cheaper for wide layers.
  • Inverted bottleneck: expand with 1×11\times11×1, depthwise 3×33\times33×3, project back; skip connects the narrow ends.
  • Compound scaling: depth, width, and resolution scaled together by one knob ϕ\phiϕ.
  • FLOPs are a proxy; measure latency on the target hardware.
  • Squeeze-and-excitation style channel attention is a cheap add-on in several efficient families.

#Object detection

Classification outputs one label per image. Detection outputs a set of (box, class, score) triples, with a variable number of objects. That raises three problems: how to propose locations, how to match predictions to ground truth, and how to remove duplicates.

#IoU: the overlap measure

Intersection over Union between two boxes AAA and BBB:

IoU(A,B)=∣A∩B∣∣A∪B∣=∣A∩B∣∣A∣+∣B∣−∣A∩B∣\text{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|}IoU(A,B)=∣A∪B∣∣A∩B∣​=∣A∣+∣B∣−∣A∩B∣∣A∩B∣​

Worked example: A=(0,0,10,10)A=(0,0,10,10)A=(0,0,10,10) and B=(5,5,15,15)B=(5,5,15,15)B=(5,5,15,15) in (x1,y1,x2,y2)(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)(x1​,y1​,x2​,y2​) form. The intersection is the square from (5,5)(5,5)(5,5) to (10,10)(10,10)(10,10), area 252525. Each box has area 100100100, so the union is 100+100−25=175100+100-25=175100+100−25=175 and IoU =25/175≈0.143=25/175\approx0.143=25/175≈0.143. IoU is 1 for identical boxes and 0 for disjoint ones. Training uses IoU thresholds to assign anchors as positive or negative (commonly around 0.5 to 0.7 for positive, below about 0.3 to 0.4 for negative, with variations by method), and evaluation uses a threshold to call a detection correct. Boxes are usually regressed as offsets relative to an anchor or grid cell, and IoU-based losses (GIoU, DIoU, CIoU) give gradients even when boxes do not overlap, which plain IoU cannot.

#Two-stage detectors: the R-CNN family

  1. R-CNN: run a classical region proposer (about 2,000 boxes), crop and warp each, run a CNN on every crop. Accurate for its time, very slow.
  2. Fast R-CNN: run the CNN once on the whole image, then pool a fixed-size feature (RoI pooling) for each proposal. Proposals are still external.
  3. Faster R-CNN: replace the external proposer with a learned Region Proposal Network (RPN) that shares the backbone. Stage one proposes class-agnostic regions ("objectness" plus box refinement); stage two classifies each region and refines the box.
  4. Mask R-CNN: adds a mask head per region for instance segmentation, and replaces RoI pooling with RoIAlign to avoid quantization error.

Strength: strong localization and accuracy, especially for small objects and with feature pyramids. Cost: two sequential stages, so higher latency.

#One-stage detectors: YOLO and RetinaNet

One-stage detectors skip proposals and predict boxes and classes directly at every location of one or more feature maps, in a single pass. YOLO divides the image into a grid and each cell predicts boxes, an objectness score, and class probabilities; later versions use multi-scale feature maps and more refined heads. SSD predicts from several feature-map scales. RetinaNet is a one-stage detector with a feature pyramid and a focal loss (below), designed to close the accuracy gap with two-stage methods.

The typical trade-off is speed for some accuracy, though the gap has narrowed a lot with modern designs. Choose by latency budget and object size distribution, not by family name.

#Anchors vs anchor-free

An anchor is a predefined box (a set of scales and aspect ratios at each location). The network predicts whether each anchor contains an object and a small offset to fix its shape. Anchors give a good starting guess, but they add hyperparameters (sizes, ratios, matching thresholds) and create a huge number of mostly-negative candidates, which is where the class imbalance below comes from.

Anchor-free detectors drop the template boxes. Two common styles are keypoint-based (predict object centers, then regress width and height from that point) and per-location (every location predicts distances to the four box sides, plus a "centerness" or quality score). Transformer-based detectors (DETR) go further: they predict a fixed-size set of boxes and use bipartite (Hungarian) matching to the ground truth during training, which removes NMS at inference at the cost of slower convergence in the original formulation.

#Non-maximum suppression (NMS)

Dense predictors fire multiple times on the same object. Greedy NMS keeps the best box and discards its near-duplicates:

  1. Sort boxes by score.
  2. Keep the top box; remove every remaining box of the same class with IoU above a threshold τ\tauτ with it.
  3. Repeat on what remains.

Worked example with τ=0.5\tau=0.5τ=0.5.   • Keep b0b_0b0​. b1b_1b1​ (0.681) and b3b_3b3​ (0.833) both exceed 0.5, so both are suppressed.
  • Next remaining is b2b_2b2​; nothing left overlaps it. Keep it.

#Average precision and mAP, defined precisely

Detection is scored per class, then averaged. For one class at IoU threshold ttt:

  1. Sort all detections across the dataset by confidence, descending.
  2. Walk down the list. A detection is a true positive (TP) if its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth box of that class is at least ttt (each ground-truth box can be matched once); otherwise it is a false positive (FP). Duplicates of an already-matched object are FPs.
  3. At each rank compute cumulative precision=TP/(TP+FP)\text{precision}=\text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FP})precision=TP/(TP+FP) and recall=TP/G\text{recall}=\text{TP}/Grecall=TP/G, where GGG is the number of ground-truth boxes.
  4. AP is the area under the precision-recall curve, after making precision monotone non-increasing (each point takes the maximum precision at any recall at or beyond it).

mAP is AP averaged over classes. Variants differ in how they handle IoU: one benchmark reports AP at a single threshold of 0.5, another averages AP over thresholds 0.50,0.55,…,0.950.50, 0.55,\dots,0.950.50,0.55,…,0.95, which rewards tight localization. State which one is meant, because the numbers differ substantially.

#Focal loss and the class-imbalance link

A dense detector evaluates tens of thousands of candidate locations per image, and nearly all are background. Plain cross-entropy (see Cross-Entropy Loss) sums over all of them, so thousands of easy negatives, each with a tiny loss, collectively dominate the gradient. Focal loss down-weights well-classified examples:

FL(pt)=−αt (1−pt)γ log⁡pt\text{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t\,(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\,\log p_tFL(pt​)=−αt​(1−pt​)γlogpt​

where ptp_tpt​ is the model's probability for the true class, γ≥0\gamma\ge0γ≥0 is the focusing parameter (a typical value is 2), and αt\alpha_tαt​ is an optional class-balancing weight. At γ=0\gamma=0γ=0 it reduces to (weighted) cross-entropy. Numbers with γ=2\gamma=2γ=2 and αt=1\alpha_t=1αt​=1: an easy example with pt=0.9p_t=0.9pt​=0.9 has CE =0.105=0.105=0.105 and FL =0.12⋅0.105=0.0011=0.1^2\cdot0.105=0.0011=0.12⋅0.105=0.0011, a roughly 100-fold reduction; a hard example with pt=0.1p_t=0.1pt​=0.1 has CE =2.303=2.303=2.303 and FL =0.92⋅2.303=1.865=0.9^2\cdot2.303=1.865=0.92⋅2.303=1.865, barely touched. The easy majority is effectively muted and the hard examples drive training. See Loss Functions for where this sits among other losses and Class Imbalance for alternatives such as resampling and hard-negative mining (the approach two-stage detectors and SSD-style methods take instead).

Common mistake

Quoting "mAP" without the IoU threshold, or computing AP by counting every detection above 0.5 IoU as a TP. Duplicate detections of one object must be FPs; that is exactly why NMS and the one-match rule matter.

Interview angle

"Why did one-stage detectors lag two-stage ones at first?" Extreme foreground-background imbalance: a proposal stage filters most background before classification, while a one-stage head sees it all. Focal loss (or hard-negative mining) is the fix. A strong answer then names the remaining trade-off: latency versus localization quality.

Rapid fire — detection
  • Output: set of (box, class, score); variable size.
  • IoU = intersection area over union area; used for matching, NMS, and evaluation.
  • Two-stage: propose then classify (Faster R-CNN); one-stage: dense direct prediction (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet).
  • Anchors: predefined box templates; anchor-free: predict center and extents directly.
  • NMS removes same-class duplicates above an IoU threshold; Soft-NMS decays instead of deleting.
  • AP = area under the monotone precision-recall curve for one class; mAP averages classes (and sometimes IoU thresholds).
  • Focal loss multiplies CE by (1−pt)γ(1-p_t)^\gamma(1−pt​)γ to mute easy negatives.
  • DETR-style set prediction with Hungarian matching avoids NMS.

#U-Net

U-Net is an encoder-decoder with skip connections. The encoder is a CNN that repeatedly downsamples, building context. The decoder upsamples (transposed convolutions or interpolation plus convolution) back to input resolution. At each decoder level, the feature map from the encoder level of the same resolution is concatenated, giving the decoder both "what" (deep, coarse semantics) and "where" (shallow, fine spatial detail).

Other segmentation heads include dilated (atrous) convolutions, feature-pyramid decoders, and, for instances, Mask R-CNN's per-region mask branch.

#Dice loss and imbalance

In many tasks the target is a small fraction of pixels (a lesion, a thin road). Per-pixel cross-entropy can be satisfied by predicting background everywhere. The Dice coefficient measures overlap of prediction PPP and target GGG directly:

Dice=2 ∣P∩G∣∣P∣+∣G∣,LDice=1−2∑ipigi+ϵ∑ipi+∑igi+ϵ\text{Dice}=\frac{2\,|P\cap G|}{|P|+|G|},\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}=1-\frac{2\sum_i p_i g_i+\epsilon}{\sum_i p_i+\sum_i g_i+\epsilon}Dice=∣P∣+∣G∣2∣P∩G∣​,LDice​=1−∑i​pi​+∑i​gi​+ϵ2∑i​pi​gi​+ϵ​

Here pi∈[0,1]p_i\in[0,1]pi​∈[0,1] is the predicted foreground probability at pixel iii (soft, so the loss is differentiable), gi∈{0,1}g_i\in\{0,1\}gi​∈{0,1} the label, and ϵ\epsilonϵ a small smoothing constant. Example: prediction covers 4 pixels, target covers 5, overlap 3 gives Dice =2⋅3/(4+5)=0.667=2\cdot3/(4+5)=0.667=2⋅3/(4+5)=0.667. Dice relates to IoU by Dice=2 IoU/(1+IoU)\text{Dice}=2\,\text{IoU}/(1+\text{IoU})Dice=2IoU/(1+IoU). Because both numerator and denominator scale with foreground size, a tiny object still contributes a meaningful loss. Caveats: the gradients can be noisy on very small or empty targets, so Dice is commonly combined with cross-entropy (or focal loss), and the metric (mean IoU or Dice) should be tracked per class rather than only pixel-wise accuracy, which is dominated by background.

Rapid fire — segmentation
  • Semantic: class per pixel; instance: mask per object; panoptic: both, covering every pixel.
  • U-Net = encoder-decoder with same-resolution concatenation skips.
  • Dice =2∣P∩G∣/(∣P∣+∣G∣)=2|P\cap G|/(|P|+|G|)=2∣P∩G∣/(∣P∣+∣G∣); Dice loss is 1−1-1− soft Dice.
  • Pixel accuracy is misleading under imbalance; report mean IoU or per-class Dice.
  • Dice plus cross-entropy is a common combined loss.

#Vision Transformers

#From image to tokens

A Vision Transformer (ViT) treats an image like a sentence of patches. For a 224×224×3224\times224\times3224×224×3 image and patch size 16:

  1. Split into (224/16)2=196(224/16)^2=196(224/16)2=196 non-overlapping patches, each 16⋅16⋅3=76816\cdot16\cdot3=76816⋅16⋅3=768 numbers.
  2. Flatten each patch and apply one shared linear projection to dimension ddd (this is exactly a conv with kernel and stride 16). For d=768d=768d=768 that layer has 768⋅768+768=590,592768\cdot768+768=590{,}592768⋅768+768=590,592 parameters.
  3. Prepend a learnable class token (or pool patch outputs at the end), giving 197 tokens.
  4. Add position embeddings (learned, one vector per position), because attention is permutation-invariant and would otherwise ignore where each patch came from.
  5. Feed the sequence through standard Transformer encoder blocks (self-attention plus MLP, with residuals and layer norm), then a linear classification head on the class token.

Attention cost is quadratic in the number of tokens, O(N2d)O(N^2 d)O(N2d). Halving the patch size to 8 quadruples the tokens, from 196 to 784, and multiplies attention cost by about 16, which is why high-resolution tasks use windowed attention (Swin-style) or hierarchical designs that merge patches progressively. Details of attention itself are in Self-Attention and Transformers & Attention.

#Inductive bias and data hunger

A CNN bakes in locality and translation equivariance: neighboring pixels interact first, and the same filter applies everywhere. A ViT has far weaker priors: any patch can attend to any other from layer one, and spatial structure must be learned (position embeddings are learned, not built in). Consequences, hedged:

  • With small or medium datasets trained from scratch, CNNs often match or beat ViTs, because the prior substitutes for data.
  • With large-scale pretraining (very large labeled or web-scale image sets, or strong self-supervision), ViTs commonly match or exceed CNNs and scale well with model and data size.
  • Strong augmentation, regularization, and distillation narrow the gap at smaller scale; hybrids (conv stem, convolutional stages, or local attention windows) reintroduce some CNN priors.
  • Results depend on the training recipe, so "ViT beats CNN" claims should be read alongside how each was trained.
Property CNN ViT
Locality prior Built in Learned
Receptive field Grows with depth Global from layer one
Position handling Implicit (equivariant) Explicit position embeddings
Small-data from scratch Usually stronger Usually weaker
Large-scale pretraining Strong Strong, often scales better

Interview angle

A good answer to "ViT or CNN?" is conditional: data volume, compute budget, latency target, and whether a good pretrained checkpoint exists. For a small labeled dataset, fine-tuning a pretrained model of either family usually beats training from scratch, so the practical question is which pretrained checkpoint fits the deployment constraints.

Rapid fire — ViT
  • Patchify, linearly embed, add position embeddings, run a Transformer encoder.
  • 2242224^22242 image, patch 16 gives 196 patches (197 with a class token).
  • Attention is O(N2)O(N^2)O(N2) in tokens; windowed or hierarchical variants handle high resolution.
  • Weaker built-in priors than CNNs, so data hunger shows up most when training from scratch.
  • Position embeddings are needed because attention is permutation-invariant.

#Self-supervised and contrastive pretraining

Labels are the expensive part. Self-supervised learning builds a training signal from the data itself. The dominant vision recipe is contrastive: pull together representations of two views of the same image, push apart views of different images.

#SimCLR-style InfoNCE

Take a batch of NNN images. Make two random augmented views of each (crop, flip, color jitter, blur), giving 2N2N2N views. Encode each with a backbone plus a small projection head to get unit-norm vectors zzz. For a positive pair (i,j)(i,j)(i,j) (two views of one image), the loss is

ℓi,j=−log⁡exp⁡(sim(zi,zj)/τ)∑k≠iexp⁡(sim(zi,zk)/τ)\ell_{i,j}=-\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp(\text{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau)}ℓi,j​=−log∑k=i​exp(sim(zi​,zk​)/τ)exp(sim(zi​,zj​)/τ)​

where sim\text{sim}sim is cosine similarity and τ\tauτ is a temperature. This is a softmax cross-entropy over 2N−12N-12N−1 candidates, where the correct "class" is the partner view: one positive and 2N−22N-22N−2 negatives. With N=256N=256N=256 that is 510 negatives per anchor. The total loss averages over all 2N2N2N anchors.

What drives behavior:

  • Negatives from the batch: larger batches give more (and harder) negatives, which is why early recipes used very large batches; momentum-encoder and queue methods (MoCo) decouple negative count from batch size.
  • Temperature τ\tauτ: small values sharpen the softmax and focus on hard negatives; too small is unstable.
  • Augmentations define invariance: crops plus color jitter teach the model to ignore position and color shifts. The choice of augmentation is the real "label".
  • Projection head: the loss is applied after a small MLP, while the representation used downstream is typically the layer before it.
  • False negatives: two different images of the same class are treated as negatives, a built-in noise source. Non-contrastive methods (BYOL-, DINO-, masked-image-modeling style) avoid explicit negatives and work well in practice.

#CLIP: dual encoders, text as supervision

CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder jointly on a large set of (image, caption) pairs. In a batch of NNN pairs, compute the N×NN\times NN×N matrix of similarities between every image and every caption; the matching pairs lie on the diagonal. The loss is a symmetric InfoNCE: cross-entropy over each row (image to text) and each column (text to image), with a learned temperature.

Zero-shot classification. To classify with no task-specific training, write a text prompt per class ("a photo of a {class}"), embed each, embed the image, and pick the class whose text embedding has the highest cosine similarity. The class names are the classifier weights. Prompt wording matters, and ensembling several templates usually helps. Limits: performance drops on fine-grained or specialized domains absent from the captions, counting and spatial relations are weak, and web-caption data carries biases.

Because both modalities land in one shared space, the same embeddings power image-text retrieval with nearest-neighbor search (see Embeddings & Vector Search), and the image encoder is a common frozen feature extractor for downstream tasks, including as the vision input of multimodal language models. The text tower is a Transformer (see Transformers & Attention); the image tower can be a ResNet or a ViT.

Memory hook

SimCLR: "which view is my twin?" CLIP: "which caption is mine?" Both are a softmax over in-batch candidates with a temperature.

Rapid fire — self-supervised and CLIP
  • Contrastive learning pulls positives together and pushes in-batch negatives apart.
  • InfoNCE is cross-entropy over one positive and 2N−22N-22N−2 negatives per anchor (SimCLR).
  • Augmentation choice defines the invariances learned.
  • CLIP: two encoders, N×NN\times NN×N similarity matrix, symmetric cross-entropy, learned temperature.
  • Zero-shot: embed class prompts as text, choose the highest cosine similarity.
  • Non-contrastive and masked-modeling methods avoid explicit negatives.

#Transfer learning and fine-tuning recipes

Most production vision work starts from a pretrained backbone. The recipe depends on dataset size and how close the new domain is to the pretraining data.

Situation Typical recipe Why
Tiny data, similar domain Freeze backbone, train a new linear head (linear probe), possibly on cached features Little to overfit; cheapest
Small data, somewhat different Train head first, then unfreeze top blocks with a small learning rate Adapts high-level features, keeps general low-level ones
Medium to large data Fine-tune everything with a low backbone learning rate and a larger head learning rate Full adaptation, still benefits from the initialization
Very different domain (e.g. medical, satellite) Fine-tune all layers, consider domain-specific or self-supervised pretraining ImageNet-style features transfer less
Large model, limited compute or many tasks Parameter-efficient methods (adapters, LoRA) Fewer trainable parameters; see [[lora-and-peft

Practical details:

  • Match preprocessing to the checkpoint: input size, normalization mean and standard deviation, channel order. A mismatch silently degrades results.
  • Differential learning rates (small for the backbone, larger for the new head) and warmup protect pretrained features from large early gradient noise.
  • Layer-wise learning-rate decay is a common ViT recipe: lower layers get smaller rates.
  • Batch norm with tiny batches during fine-tuning is unstable; freezing norm statistics or switching to a group- or layer-norm model is a common remedy (see BatchNorm vs LayerNorm).
  • Higher fine-tuning resolution than pretraining often helps (position embeddings are interpolated for ViTs).
  • Evaluate with a proper held-out split, since fine-tuning on a small set overfits quickly; see Overfitting & Underfitting and Training Neural Networks.

#Data augmentation

Augmentation is a regularizer that encodes invariances you believe the task has. It is applied to training data only, not to the validation or test set (aside from deterministic resizing and cropping).

Family Examples Notes
Geometric Random crop and resize, flip, small rotation, scale Horizontal flip is wrong for text or for tasks where left and right matter
Photometric Brightness, contrast, color jitter, blur, noise Match expected lighting and sensor variation
Erasing Cutout, random erasing Forces use of multiple cues
Mixing Mixup (blend two images and labels), CutMix (paste a patch, mix labels by area) Strong regularizers; soften labels
Learned policies AutoAugment, RandAugment (random ops with a global magnitude knob) Fewer hyperparameters than hand-design

Rules of thumb: for detection and segmentation, geometric transforms must be applied jointly to the image and its boxes or masks. Augmentation strength should scale with the risk of overfitting (small data, large model), and a label-changing augmentation (a vertical flip of a "6") breaks the task. ViTs trained from scratch typically need stronger augmentation and regularization than CNNs. Mixed-label methods change calibration, so check it if probabilities matter (see Calibration). Other regularizers are covered in Regularization and Dropout.

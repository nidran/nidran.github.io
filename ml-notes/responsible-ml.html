Responsible ML: Fairness, Explainability & Privacy

A model that scores well on aggregate metrics can still be unfair to a subgroup, impossible to explain, or leak the data it was trained on. These are engineering properties to specify, measure, and monitor.

Responsible ML covers three overlapping concerns: fairness (does the system treat groups comparably?), explainability (can people understand why it produced an output?), and privacy (does training and serving expose individuals?). In interviews they appear as system design follow-ups ("how would you make sure this does not discriminate?", "how would you explain a rejection?", "can your model leak training data?"). Good answers define the metric, say where in the pipeline the problem enters, name the trade-off, and describe how it is monitored after launch. Regulatory and legal points here are stated only generally and are not legal advice; requirements differ by jurisdiction and domain, so a real system needs input from legal and compliance specialists.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Define demographic parity, equalized odds, equal opportunity, and calibration within groups, and compute them from a confusion matrix.
  • State the fairness impossibility result with its conditions and explain what it implies for metric choice.
  • Locate where bias enters (data, labels, features, objective, deployment) and pick pre-, in-, or post-processing mitigations with their costs.
  • Explain permutation importance, PDP/ICE, SHAP, and LIME, including their assumptions and failure modes, and why attention is not automatically an explanation.
  • Describe k-anonymity's limits, differential privacy with epsilon, federated learning, and membership inference.
  • Outline model cards, fairness monitoring, and LLM-specific risks.

#Fairness definitions

Let Y^\hat YY^ be the model's decision, YYY the true outcome, and AAA a sensitive attribute (group membership). Different definitions encode different ideas of fairness; they are not interchangeable.

Criterion Requirement Intuition
Demographic (statistical) parity P(Y^=1∣A=a)=P(Y^=1∣A=b)P(\hat Y=1 \mid A=a) = P(\hat Y=1 \mid A=b)P(Y^=1∣A=a)=P(Y^=1∣A=b) Same selection rate across groups, regardless of true outcomes
Equal opportunity P(Y^=1∣Y=1,A=a)=P(Y^=1∣Y=1,A=b)P(\hat Y=1 \mid Y=1, A=a) = P(\hat Y=1 \mid Y=1, A=b)P(Y^=1∣Y=1,A=a)=P(Y^=1∣Y=1,A=b) Qualified people are accepted at equal rates (equal true positive rate)

Demographic parity ignores the label, so it can force acceptance of unqualified candidates in one group or reject qualified ones in another when true rates differ. It fits cases where the outcome label itself is suspect. Equal opportunity and equalized odds condition on the true label and so assume the label is a trustworthy measure of the outcome. Calibration within groups is natural for probabilistic scores (see Calibration), since a risk score is only useful for decisions if it means the same thing for everyone.

#Worked example: metrics from confusion matrices

Two groups of 100 people each, with a binary decision:

Group TP FP FN TN
A 40 10 10 40
B 15 20 5 60

Computed values (checked with a short script):

Metric Group A Group B Gap
Base rate P(Y=1)P(Y=1)P(Y=1) 0.50 0.20 0.30
Selection rate 0.50 0.35 0.15
TPR (recall) 0.80 0.75 0.05
FPR 0.20 0.25 0.05
PPV (precision) 0.80 0.43 0.37
Accuracy 0.80 0.75 0.05

Reading it: demographic parity is violated by 15 points; equal opportunity is off by 5 points; equalized odds is off by 5 points on each rate; and precision differs sharply (0.80 vs 0.43), so a positive decision is far less reliable for group B. The model's error rates are similar across groups, yet precision diverges. That gap is a mechanical consequence of different base rates, which leads to the impossibility result.

Memory hook

Parity looks at decisions, odds look at errors given the truth, calibration looks at the truth given the score.

#The impossibility result

Several results (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan; Chouldechova) show that fairness criteria conflict. A precise statement for a binary classifier: when the base rates differ between groups, P(Y=1∣A=a)≠P(Y=1∣A=b)P(Y=1 \mid A=a) \ne P(Y=1 \mid A=b)P(Y=1∣A=a)=P(Y=1∣A=b), and the classifier is not perfect, it is not possible to have equal PPV (predictive parity) and equal FPR and equal FNR across groups simultaneously. The identity behind it, for group prevalence ppp:

FPR=p1−p⋅1−PPVPPV⋅(1−FNR)\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot(1-\text{FNR})FPR=1−pp​⋅PPV1−PPV​⋅(1−FNR)

If PPV and FNR are equal across groups but ppp differs, FPR must differ. For group B above: 0.20.8×0.5710.429×0.75=0.25\frac{0.2}{0.8} \times \frac{0.571}{0.429} \times 0.75 = 0.250.80.2​×0.4290.571​×0.75=0.25, matching the table. The score-based version (Kleinberg et al.) says that calibration within groups and balance for the positive and negative classes (equal average score among true positives, and among true negatives, across groups) cannot all hold unless base rates are equal or prediction is perfect.

Conditions to state in an interview: the result needs unequal base rates and imperfect prediction. It does not say fairness is impossible in general, and it vanishes if base rates are equal or if the classifier is perfect. Its practical meaning is that choosing a fairness criterion is a value judgement about which error matters, to be made with domain, policy, and legal input, and then documented. For lending-style risk scores, calibration is often prioritized; for screening or access-to-opportunity settings, equal opportunity is common; when the label reflects historical discrimination, parity-style targets may be defended.

Interview angle

Weak: "Make the model fair by removing the sensitive attribute." Strong: "Dropping the attribute often leaves the signal in place because proxies (postal code, school, browsing patterns) carry it. Pick the metric tied to the harm, measure it across groups using held-out data with sensitive attributes, and discuss the trade-off, noting that with unequal base rates and an imperfect classifier calibration and equal error rates cannot hold together."

#Where bias enters the pipeline

Bias is not a single bug in the model; it can enter at each stage.

  • Problem framing and target choice. A proxy label (arrests instead of offences, spend instead of need, clicks instead of satisfaction) carries the measurement process's bias.
  • Data collection and sampling. Under-representation, convenience samples, and survivorship (only approved applicants have repayment labels) distort what the model sees. See Class Imbalance for the minority-group cousin.
  • Labels. Human annotators and historical decisions encode prior judgement. Label noise can be group-dependent.
  • Features. Proxies for protected attributes; features that are measured with different quality across groups (a sensor, a language, a lighting condition).
  • Objective and metrics. Optimizing average accuracy lets the majority group dominate; a single threshold on a score with different distributions per group yields different error rates.
  • Evaluation. Aggregate metrics hide subgroup failures; test sets that mirror the training distribution inherit its gaps.
  • Deployment and feedback loops. Model decisions shape future data (who gets shown, who gets loans), reinforcing initial skews. See Data Drift for shifts that affect groups unevenly.
  • Interface and human use. Automation bias, or users overriding the model differently by group.
Common mistake

Treating fairness as a post-training audit. Many causes sit upstream (labels, sampling, target definition) and cannot be repaired by threshold tweaks alone.

#Mitigation: pre-, in-, and post-processing

Stage Methods Strengths Costs and risks
Pre-processing Re-sampling, re-weighting, relabelling, learning fair representations, collecting more data for under-served groups Model-agnostic; addresses data causes May discard signal; representations can still leak group information
In-processing Fairness constraints or penalty terms in the loss, adversarial debiasing (a head tries to predict AAA from representations), group-robust objectives (minimize worst-group loss) Directly optimizes the trade-off Training complexity; constraints may not generalize off the training distribution
Post-processing Group-specific thresholds, score adjustment to equalize TPR/FPR Works on a fixed model; easy to audit Uses the sensitive attribute at decision time (may be restricted); can reduce accuracy; ties to the chosen metric

Common trade-offs and caveats:

  • Accuracy vs fairness. Constraints usually cost some aggregate accuracy, but the cost may be small, and when group data quality differs, improving data can improve both.
  • Fairness through unawareness (removing AAA) tends to fail because of proxies, and prevents measurement of disparities.
  • Group metrics are not individual fairness. Similar individuals should receive similar outcomes; group parity can still treat individuals inconsistently.
  • Intersectionality. Fairness across gender and age separately does not guarantee fairness for each combination; sample sizes get small, so use confidence intervals.
  • Feedback. A mitigation tested on historical data can shift behaviour after deployment, so re-measure in production.
Rapid fire: fairness
  • Demographic parity compares selection rates and ignores labels.
  • Equal opportunity compares TPR; equalized odds compares TPR and FPR.
  • Calibration within groups: the score means the same thing for every group.
  • With unequal base rates and an imperfect classifier, calibration/predictive parity and equal FPR/FNR cannot all hold.
  • Removing the sensitive attribute does not remove proxies and blocks auditing.
  • Pre-processing fixes data, in-processing changes the loss, post-processing adjusts decisions.
  • Report per-group metrics with confidence intervals; small groups give noisy estimates.
  • The choice of fairness metric is a policy decision, not only a technical one.

#Explainability

Why explain. Debugging (finding leakage or spurious cues), trust and adoption, recourse for affected people, regulatory expectations about meaningful information on automated decisions (stated generally), and detecting fairness problems.

Global vs local. A global explanation describes the model overall (which features matter on average, the shape of a relationship). A local explanation describes one prediction (why this applicant was declined). Intrinsically interpretable models (sparse linear models, small trees, generalized additive models) provide explanations by construction; post-hoc methods approximate explanations for black boxes. For high-stakes settings, a well-tuned interpretable model is often competitive and worth trying first.

#Permutation importance

Shuffle one feature's values on held-out data and measure the drop in the metric. A large drop means the model relies on that feature. It is model-agnostic and simple, but with correlated features the model can fall back on the correlated twin so importance is split or understated, and shuffling creates unrealistic rows. It measures reliance of the model, not causal importance in the world. Compute it on held-out data, since on training data it can reward overfit features. Compare with impurity-based importance of tree ensembles, which is biased toward high-cardinality features.

#PDP and ICE

A partial dependence plot averages the prediction over the data while sweeping one feature across its range:

PDj(xj)=EX−j[f^(xj,X−j)]\text{PD}_j(x_j) = \mathbb{E}_{X_{-j}}\bigl[\hat f(x_j, X_{-j})\bigr]PDj​(xj​)=EX−j​​[f^​(xj​,X−j​)]

where X−jX_{-j}X−j​ are the other features. An ICE (individual conditional expectation) plot draws one curve per instance. PDP assumes the swept feature is independent of the others; with correlation it evaluates the model on implausible combinations (for example age 20 with 30 years of tenure). Averaging can also hide heterogeneity, such as opposite slopes for two subgroups canceling to a flat line, which ICE reveals. Accumulated local effects (ALE) plots handle correlation better.

#SHAP in depth

SHAP assigns each feature a Shapley value from cooperative game theory: its average marginal contribution to the prediction over all orderings of feature "arrival". For a prediction f(x)f(x)f(x), with baseline ϕ0\phi_0ϕ0​ (the expected prediction) and MMM features:

f(x)=ϕ0+∑j=1Mϕj,ϕj=∑S⊆F∖{j}∣S∣! (M−∣S∣−1)!M![v(S∪{j})−v(S)]f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{M}\phi_j, \qquad \phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq F\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!\,(M-|S|-1)!}{M!}\Bigl[v(S\cup\{j\}) - v(S)\Bigr]f(x)=ϕ0​+j=1∑M​ϕj​,ϕj​=S⊆F∖{j}∑​M!∣S∣!(M−∣S∣−1)!​[v(S∪{j})−v(S)]

Here FFF is the set of all features and v(S)v(S)v(S) is the model's expected output when only the features in SSS are known. The sum over subsets is the weighting that makes the attribution satisfy efficiency (contributions add up to the prediction minus baseline), symmetry, dummy (a feature that never changes the output gets zero), and additivity. Exact computation is exponential, so implementations use TreeSHAP (polynomial-time for tree ensembles), KernelSHAP (a sampled weighted regression), and DeepSHAP-style approximations.

Assumptions and failure modes to know:

  • How "missing" is defined. v(S)v(S)v(S) requires marginalizing out features not in SSS. The interventional (marginal) version samples absent features independently of present ones, which can create unrealistic inputs under correlation; the conditional version respects correlations but can attribute credit to features the model does not use, because they are informative about used ones. Neither is universally right; the choice should match the question.
  • Background dataset matters. Attributions are relative to a baseline distribution; changing it changes the numbers.
  • Not causal. SHAP explains the model, not the world. A high SHAP value says the model leans on a feature, not that changing it changes the outcome.
  • Correlated features share credit in ways that can mislead feature selection.
  • Additive local attributions can hide interactions; SHAP interaction values address pairs.
  • Manipulability. Adversarially constructed models can behave fairly on the perturbed samples used by sampling-based explainers while remaining biased on real data.
  • Cost and instability. KernelSHAP is expensive and sampling variance can reorder close features.
  • Units. For classifiers, values for tree-ensemble classifiers are often in log-odds (raw margin) rather than probability; interpret accordingly.

Global importance from SHAP is usually the mean absolute value 1n∑i∣ϕj(i)∣\frac{1}{n}\sum_i |\phi_j^{(i)}|n1​∑i​∣ϕj(i)​∣ over a dataset; beeswarm plots add direction.

#LIME in depth

LIME explains one prediction by fitting a simple local surrogate (typically a sparse linear model) to the black box's behaviour in a neighbourhood of the instance. It samples perturbed points around xxx, queries the model, weights samples by proximity, and fits:

ξ(x)=arg⁡min⁡g∈G L(f,g,πx)+Ω(g)\xi(x) = \arg\min_{g \in G}\ \mathcal{L}(f, g, \pi_x) + \Omega(g)ξ(x)=argg∈Gmin​ L(f,g,πx​)+Ω(g)

where fff is the model, ggg a simple interpretable model from family GGG, πx\pi_xπx​ the proximity kernel around xxx, and Ω(g)\Omega(g)Ω(g) a complexity penalty (for example, number of non-zero weights).

Failure modes:

  • Instability. Results depend on random sampling; repeated runs can give different top features.
  • Neighbourhood definition. The kernel width is a hyperparameter with no principled default; explanations can change substantially with it.
  • Unrealistic perturbations. Perturbing tabular features independently, or masking words or superpixels, creates off-manifold inputs where the model's behaviour is not representative.
  • Local linearity. If the decision boundary is strongly nonlinear in the neighbourhood, the surrogate is a poor fit; check its fidelity (local R2R^2R2).
  • No consistency guarantees. Unlike SHAP, LIME has no efficiency or consistency axioms, and can be fooled like other perturbation-based methods.

A practical stance: use SHAP for tree models where TreeSHAP is exact and fast; use LIME-style or KernelSHAP methods when only black-box access is available (gradient-based methods when gradients are accessible), and verify explanations with sanity checks (do they change when the model is retrained on randomized labels?).

Go deeper (optional)

Counterfactual explanations answer "what is the smallest change that would flip the decision?", which is often more actionable for recourse than feature attributions. They require constraints on actionability (age cannot be reduced) and plausibility, and the set of valid counterfactuals is rarely unique.

#Attention is not explanation

In transformers, attention weights show where information is mixed, and are tempting to display as "what the model looked at". Studies have shown that attention weights can be altered substantially without changing predictions, that different attention patterns can yield the same outputs, and that weights often correlate poorly with gradient-based importance. Reasons include: values are mixed after weighting (a high weight on a token with a near-zero value vector contributes little), many layers and heads combine in complex ways, and residual paths bypass attention. Attention can still be a useful diagnostic, but claiming it as a faithful explanation needs supporting evidence from perturbation or attribution methods. See Self-Attention for the mechanism.

Interview angle

A strong answer separates faithfulness (does the explanation reflect what the model actually computes?) from plausibility (does it look reasonable to a human?). A plausible but unfaithful explanation is worse than none, because it creates unearned trust.

Rapid fire: explainability
  • Global explains the model overall; local explains one prediction.
  • Permutation importance: shuffle on held-out data; correlated features distort it.
  • PDP averages over the others and assumes independence; ICE shows heterogeneity.
  • SHAP: Shapley values, additive and efficient, relative to a background baseline.
  • SHAP and LIME explain the model, not causal effects in the world.
  • LIME is a local sparse surrogate; it is sensitive to the sampling kernel and to off-manifold perturbations.
  • Attention weights are not guaranteed to be faithful explanations.
  • Consider interpretable models first when the stakes are high.

#Privacy

#PII handling

Practical measures include data minimization (collect and retain only what is needed), purpose limitation, access controls and audit logs, encryption in transit and at rest, retention limits with deletion workflows, separation of identifiers from features (tokenization, pseudonymization), and care with free-text fields, logs, and embeddings, which can contain personal data. Pseudonymization is not anonymization, since identifiers can often be re-linked with auxiliary data. Many privacy regimes include deletion or access rights, so the pipeline needs lineage: knowing which datasets and models used a given person's data. Whether and how a trained model must be changed after a deletion request is a legal and technical question; options range from periodic retraining to machine unlearning research methods.

#k-anonymity and its limits

A release is k-anonymous if every combination of quasi-identifiers (age band, postcode, gender) appears for at least kkk records, so each person hides among at least k−1k-1k−1 others. Limits:

  • Homogeneity. If all kkk records in a group share the same sensitive value, it is revealed anyway; l-diversity and t-closeness add constraints on sensitive-value distribution.
  • Background knowledge. An attacker who knows extra facts narrows the group.
  • Composition. Several independently anonymized releases of overlapping data can be joined to re-identify people.
  • High dimensionality. With many attributes, almost every record is unique, so generalization destroys utility.
  • Well-known re-identification of "anonymized" datasets by linking with public records illustrates these limits.

#Differential privacy

Differential privacy (DP) bounds how much any single person's data can change the output distribution. A randomized mechanism M\mathcal{M}M is (ε,δ)(\varepsilon,\delta)(ε,δ)-differentially private if for all datasets D,D′D, D'D,D′ differing in one person's record and all output sets SSS:

P[M(D)∈S]≤eε P[M(D′)∈S]+δP[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,P[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \deltaP[M(D)∈S]≤eεP[M(D′)∈S]+δ

Intuition: an observer of the output cannot tell with confidence whether any particular person was in the dataset. Smaller ε\varepsilonε means stronger privacy and more noise; ε\varepsilonε near 0 is close to "the output is independent of any individual", while large values (tens) give weak guarantees. δ\deltaδ is a small failure probability, set well below 1/n1/n1/n. Noise is calibrated to the sensitivity (the maximum effect of one record), for example Laplace noise with scale Δ/ε\Delta/\varepsilonΔ/ε for counts. Privacy loss composes: repeated queries consume a privacy budget.

In ML, DP-SGD clips each example's gradient to norm CCC and adds Gaussian noise to the summed gradients before the update, with an accountant tracking total ε\varepsilonε. Costs: lower accuracy (worse for small datasets, rare classes, and complex models), slower training, more tuning, and disproportionate accuracy loss for under-represented groups, which links privacy back to fairness. DP protects against inference about individuals in the training set; it does not protect against a model being wrong or biased.

#Federated learning at a glance

In federated learning, devices or institutions train locally and send model updates (not raw data) to a server that aggregates them (for example federated averaging). It reduces centralization of raw data but is not private by itself: updates can leak information (gradient inversion), so it is usually combined with secure aggregation and differential privacy. Challenges include non-IID data across clients, communication cost, stragglers and dropouts, and difficulty debugging with no direct access to data.

#Membership inference and other attacks

Membership inference tries to determine whether a specific record was in the training set, typically exploiting that models are more confident or have lower loss on training examples. Overfitting and memorization raise vulnerability (though well-generalized models can still leak), so regularization and early stopping can reduce risk and DP gives a formal bound (see Generalization). Related attacks: model inversion (reconstructing representative inputs from outputs), training-data extraction (large generative models can emit memorized sequences), and model stealing via query access. Defenses: limit output detail (labels rather than full probabilities where possible, which reduces but does not eliminate the risk), rate-limit queries, deduplicate training data, apply DP, and test with membership-inference audits before release.

Rapid fire: privacy
  • Pseudonymized data is not anonymous; linkage can re-identify.
  • k-anonymity fails under homogeneity, background knowledge, composition, and high dimensions.
  • DP: a single record changes output probabilities by at most a factor eεe^{\varepsilon}eε (plus δ\deltaδ).
  • Smaller ε\varepsilonε means more privacy and more noise; budgets compose across queries.
  • DP-SGD: per-example clipping plus Gaussian noise; utility cost is larger for rare groups.
  • Federated learning keeps raw data local but still needs secure aggregation and DP for strong protection.
  • Membership inference mostly exploits overfitting and memorization; regularization helps and DP bounds it.

#Model documentation: model cards

A model card is a short structured document that travels with a model. Typical contents:

  • Intended use and out-of-scope uses.
  • Training data summary: sources, time range, known gaps, sensitive attributes available for auditing.
  • Evaluation data and metrics, disaggregated by relevant subgroups and conditions, with confidence intervals.
  • Known limitations, failure modes, and caveats such as distribution shift sensitivity.
  • Fairness criteria chosen, with the reasoning for the choice and the trade-off accepted.
  • Ethical considerations, privacy measures, and contact or escalation path.
  • Version, owners, and re-evaluation schedule.

Companion artefacts include datasheets for datasets (provenance, collection, labelling process) and risk assessments before launch. The value of the card is the discipline of stating what the model is for and when it should not be used; as a deliverable it is also checked by reviewers who were not on the team.

#Monitoring fairness in production

Offline fairness checks are a snapshot; production drift can reintroduce gaps. See ML in Production and Data Drift for the general monitoring setup. Fairness-specific points:

  • Per-group metrics over time. Track selection rate, and (when labels arrive) TPR, FPR, precision, and calibration by group, with alerts on gaps exceeding thresholds and on confidence intervals rather than point estimates.
  • Delayed and biased labels. Outcomes may arrive late or only for accepted cases (selective labels), so monitor proxies, run randomized holdouts or exploration when permissible, and consider reject-inference methods.
  • Input drift by segment. A feature pipeline failure that affects one language or region shows up as segment-level drift long before aggregate metrics move; compare with Training-Serving Skew.
  • Sensitive attribute availability. Measuring disparity requires the attribute or a proxy; collecting it has its own privacy and legal considerations, so approaches include voluntary self-reported data held separately, and estimated proxies with documented error.
  • Slice discovery. Beyond predefined groups, search for under-performing slices automatically.
  • Intervention plan. Decide beforehand what happens when a threshold trips: rollback, fallback rule, human review, retraining.
  • Feedback loops. Watch for changes in who the system shows or serves that alter future training data.

#LLM-specific risks

Generative systems add risks that classic fairness metrics do not capture.

  • Hallucination. Fluent but unsupported or false statements. Mitigations include grounding with retrieval (see RAG), citations, abstention, and verification steps; see Hallucination for causes and mitigations in depth.
  • Prompt injection. Instructions hidden in user input or in retrieved content (a web page, an email, a document) that the model follows as if they came from the developer. Direct injection comes from the user; indirect injection arrives via tools or retrieval. It is hard to eliminate because instructions and data share a channel. Layered defenses include least-privilege tool access, treating retrieved text as untrusted data, confirmation for side-effecting actions, output filtering, separating privileged and unprivileged contexts, and red-teaming.
  • Data leakage. Training-data memorization, system-prompt disclosure, retrieval over documents the user should not see (missing access control in the index), and sensitive data in logs or prompts sent to third-party services. Mitigations: enforce document-level permissions at retrieval time, redact PII before indexing and logging, deduplicate training data, and cap what the model can echo.
  • Bias and toxicity. Stereotyped outputs and uneven quality across languages or dialects; evaluate with targeted prompt sets, per-group comparisons, and human review.
  • Over-reliance and misuse. Confident wrong answers encourage over-trust; communicate uncertainty and keep humans in the loop for high-stakes actions.
  • Evaluation. Safety and fairness need dedicated test suites, adversarial prompts, and continuing production sampling; see LLM Evaluation for judges, benchmarks, and their limits, and LLM Training & Alignment for how preference tuning shapes refusals and its side effects.
Common mistake

Assuming a content filter on the output covers prompt injection. An injected instruction can cause a harmful tool call or data exfiltration that never appears in the visible text, so controls belong on actions and permissions as well as on text.

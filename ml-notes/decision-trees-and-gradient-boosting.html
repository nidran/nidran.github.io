Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting

Classical Models: from a single CART tree to forests to XGBoost-style boosting.

Classification Methods and Bagging vs Boosting give the headline contrast (bagging reduces variance, boosting reduces bias). This chapter goes underneath it: how a tree actually chooses splits, why a single tree is unstable, what random forests really average, how gradient boosting is functional gradient descent, and how the major libraries differ in practice. These are the topics where senior interviewers probe for mechanism rather than slogans.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Compute Gini, entropy, information gain, and variance reduction for a split, and say when they differ.
  • Explain cost-complexity pruning, and how trees handle categorical and missing values.
  • Describe OOB error and why impurity-based importance is biased.
  • Derive gradient boosting as gradient descent in function space, and write the second-order XGBoost leaf weight and gain.
  • Compare XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost on split finding, tree growth, and categorical handling.
  • Give a tuning order, explain early stopping, and say when GBDT tends to beat deep nets on tabular data (and when it may not).

#CART: how a tree chooses a split

A CART (Classification And Regression Tree) recursively partitions feature space with binary, axis-aligned splits xj≤tx_j\le txj​≤t (or a subset test for a categorical feature). Each leaf predicts a constant: the class distribution (or majority class) for classification, the mean of yyy for regression. Growing is greedy: at each node, pick the split with the largest impurity decrease and recurse. Greedy means locally optimal, not globally optimal; finding the best tree is NP-complete.

#Split gain

For a candidate split sending nLn_LnL​ of nnn samples left and nRn_RnR​ right, the impurity decrease is

Δ=I(parent)−nLnI(left)−nRnI(right).\Delta = I(\text{parent})-\frac{n_L}{n}I(\text{left})-\frac{n_R}{n}I(\text{right}).Δ=I(parent)−nnL​​I(left)−nnR​​I(right).

With entropy as III, Δ\DeltaΔ is the information gain (the reduction in label entropy from knowing the branch). With variance as III, Δ\DeltaΔ is the variance reduction, equivalent to the reduction in sum of squared errors. Worked example: a node with 10 samples, 5 positive and 5 negative, so G=0.5G=0.5G=0.5. A split yields left (4 positive, 1 negative; G=1−0.64−0.04=0.32G=1-0.64-0.04=0.32G=1−0.64−0.04=0.32) and right (1 positive, 4 negative; G=0.32G=0.32G=0.32). Then Δ=0.5−0.5(0.32)−0.5(0.32)=0.18\Delta=0.5-0.5(0.32)-0.5(0.32)=0.18Δ=0.5−0.5(0.32)−0.5(0.32)=0.18.

Finding the best threshold. For a numeric feature, sort the values and scan the candidate thresholds (midpoints between consecutive distinct values), updating left/right class counts incrementally. That costs O(nlog⁡n)O(n\log n)O(nlogn) per feature per node for the sort, or O(n)O(n)O(n) with presorted/binned data. Histogram-based libraries bin features into at most about 255 bins first and scan bins instead, which is the reason for their speed (see below).

#Controlling size: pruning

A tree grown until leaves are pure has low bias and high variance: it memorizes noise. Two controls:

  • Pre-pruning (early stopping): limit max_depth, require min_samples_leaf or min_samples_split, require a minimum impurity decrease. Fast, but can stop too early, since a split with small immediate gain can enable a very useful later split (the XOR pattern, where no single split helps but two together separate the classes perfectly).
  • Post-pruning, cost-complexity (weakest-link) pruning: grow a large tree T0T_0T0​, then for a penalty α≥0\alpha\ge0α≥0 minimize

Rα(T)=R(T)+α ∣T∣,R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha\,|T|,Rα​(T)=R(T)+α∣T∣,

where R(T)R(T)R(T) is the training error (or impurity-weighted loss) and ∣T∣|T|∣T∣ the number of leaves. As α\alphaα increases, repeatedly collapse the internal node whose removal increases error least per leaf removed, yielding a nested sequence of subtrees; choose α\alphaα by cross-validation (the 1-standard-error rule picks the smallest tree within one SE of the best). In practice with ensembles, individual trees are controlled by depth and leaf-size limits rather than tuned pruning.

#Categorical and missing values

Categorical features.

  • A subset split on a KKK-level feature has 2K−1−12^{K-1}-12K−1−1 candidates. For binary classification and regression, a classical result shows the optimal subset split can be found by ordering the categories by mean response (or class-1 proportion) and scanning the K−1K-1K−1 split points along that order; some libraries (for example LightGBM, which sorts categories by gradient statistics, and R tree packages) exploit this. For multiclass the shortcut does not hold exactly.
  • One-hot encoding works but makes sparse features that a tree can only isolate one level at a time, which is inefficient for high cardinality and dilutes importance across dummies.
  • High cardinality raises overfitting risk: with many levels, some subset will split the training data well by chance. Splits on such features also get inflated impurity-based importance (see below).
  • Target (mean) encoding replaces a level by a statistic of the target, but if computed on the same rows it leaks the label; it must be computed out-of-fold or with ordered schemes (the CatBoost idea below), plus smoothing toward the global mean.

Missing values. Options: impute (simple, but loses the signal that data were missing); add a missingness indicator; CART-style surrogate splits (use another feature that mimics the primary split for rows where it is missing); or, as in XGBoost, LightGBM, and recent scikit-learn tree models, learn a default direction for missing values at each split by trying both and keeping the better gain. The last is convenient and exploits informative missingness, but if values are missing for a reason that differs between training and serving, the learned routing can be wrong, so verify the missingness mechanism is stable.

#Instability and other limits of single trees

  • High variance. A small change in data can change an early split and hence the whole tree below it. This is exactly why averaging (bagging) helps.
  • Piecewise-constant, axis-aligned. A tree approximates a diagonal boundary or smooth trend with a staircase and needs many splits to do it; rotating the feature space can change performance a lot (a reason that engineered linear combinations or PCA features sometimes help trees).
  • No extrapolation. A regression tree predicts a leaf mean, so outside the training range predictions are constant; trees should not be expected to follow a linear trend beyond the data (a concern in forecasting; see Time Series Stationarity).
  • Split-selection bias. Greedy impurity criteria prefer features with many possible splits (continuous or high-cardinality), which can select spurious features. Conditional inference trees correct this with significance tests; ensembles mitigate it partly.
  • Interpretability is real for shallow trees and fades quickly with depth or ensembling.
Interview angle

"Why is a single decision tree high variance?" Greedy top-down splitting makes early decisions that condition everything below, and small perturbations in the data can flip them; a deep tree also has enough capacity to fit noise. The structural fix is averaging many perturbed trees (bagging) or limiting capacity (pruning).

#Random forests in detail

A random forest (Breiman) grows BBB trees, each on a bootstrap sample, and averages predictions (or votes). Two randomization sources decorrelate the trees:

  1. Bootstrap rows per tree. Each bootstrap sample draws nnn rows with replacement; the probability a given row is absent is (1−1/n)n→e−1≈0.368(1-1/n)^n\to e^{-1}\approx0.368(1−1/n)n→e−1≈0.368, so each tree sees about 63.2%63.2\%63.2% unique rows.
  2. Random feature subset at every split (not once per tree): only mtrym_\text{try}mtry​ of the ppp features are candidates at each node. Common heuristics are about p\sqrt pp​ for classification and about p/3p/3p/3 for regression, but treat these as starting points to tune (library defaults differ).

Why the feature subsampling matters. For BBB identically distributed trees with variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 and pairwise correlation ρ\rhoρ, the variance of their average is

ρ σ2+1−ρB σ2.\rho\,\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 .ρσ2+B1−ρ​σ2.

More trees shrinks only the second term; the first, ρσ2\rho\sigma^2ρσ2, is a floor set by how correlated the trees are. If one feature is very strong, bootstrap alone leaves every tree splitting on it at the root, so ρ\rhoρ stays high; restricting candidates per split lowers ρ\rhoρ at a modest cost in each tree's strength. Lower mtrym_\text{try}mtry​ means more diversity but weaker trees; it is the central forest hyperparameter. Trees are typically grown deep (low bias), because averaging handles variance.

Out-of-bag (OOB) error. Each row is out-of-bag for about 36.8%36.8\%36.8% of the trees. Predict each row using only trees that did not train on it, and score those predictions. This gives an approximately unbiased generalization estimate without a separate validation set, which is convenient for tuning. Caveats: it assumes rows are i.i.d.; with grouped, temporal, or duplicated data the OOB estimate is optimistic because related rows land in other trees' training sets (use group-aware or forward-chaining validation instead; see Cross-Validation); and tuning heavily against OOB reintroduces optimism.

Does adding trees overfit? For a forest, increasing BBB does not increase variance in the way depth does; error generally plateaus, so more trees is safe apart from compute and memory. The forest can still overfit through very deep trees with noisy labels and tiny leaves, so min_samples_leaf is a useful regularizer.

Probabilities. Averaged leaf frequencies are often poorly calibrated, since averaging pulls extremes toward the middle; calibrate (Platt or isotonic on held-out data) if probabilities matter.

#Feature importance: what the numbers mean and where they mislead

Method What it measures Known problems
Impurity-based (MDI / "gain") Total impurity decrease from splits on the feature, averaged across trees Computed on training data, so it credits features that merely fit noise; biased toward high-cardinality or continuous features; shares credit unpredictably among correlated features
Permutation importance Drop in a chosen metric when the feature's values are shuffled (best on held-out or OOB data) Shuffling breaks correlations and can create implausible rows; correlated features can each look unimportant because the model leans on the twin; costs one prediction pass per feature per repeat
SHAP Each feature's average marginal contribution via Shapley values (below) Explains the model, not the world; sensitive to the background choice and to correlation handling
Drop-column (retrain) Change in score when the feature is removed and the model retrained Costly; reflects what the retrained model can compensate for, so redundant features look unimportant
Importance is not causation

A high importance means the model relies on the feature for prediction, not that changing the feature changes the outcome. Correlated features split importance (or one hides the other), so report grouped importances for feature clusters and confirm with an ablation before drawing conclusions.

#Second-order boosting (the XGBoost view)

Interview angle

Be ready for "what is the 'gradient' in gradient boosting?" The answer: the derivative of the loss with respect to the current predictions of each training example, which is the direction in function space that most reduces the loss. Each tree is a regression fit to that direction, and ν\nuν is the step size. Squared loss turns the gradient into the plain residual; other losses give other targets.

#Why boosting overfits differently from bagging

Each stage fits the remaining error, so after enough rounds the ensemble starts fitting noise; test error typically follows a U-shape in the number of rounds, so the number of trees is a regularization knob (unlike forests). With small ν\nuν the minimum is broader and later. Shallow trees (depth 3 to 8, depending on interactions) are preferred because each stage should be a weak, low-variance learner and because depth equals the maximum interaction order the model can express.

#XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost

All three implement regularized gradient-boosted decision trees with the same core idea; they differ in how they find splits, grow trees, and handle categoricals. Version details change over time, so treat the table as the typical design.

Histogram splits. Instead of sorting values per node, bin each feature once (about 256 bins by default in many setups) and, for each node, build a (G,H)(G,H)(G,H) histogram per feature; best split per feature is then a scan over bins, so cost scales with bins rather than rows. A child's histogram can be derived by subtracting its sibling's from the parent's, nearly halving the work. The price is quantization: thresholds are limited to bin edges, usually a negligible accuracy loss.

Leaf-wise versus level-wise. Level-wise growth expands all nodes at a depth, producing balanced trees with a bounded size by depth. Leaf-wise growth always splits the single most beneficial leaf, reaching lower loss for the same number of leaves but giving unbalanced trees that can overfit on small datasets; hence the main complexity control is num_leaves plus min_data_in_leaf, not max_depth alone. Rule of thumb: keep num_leaves well below 2max_depth2^{\text{max\_depth}}2max_depth.

CatBoost's ordered target statistics. Plain target encoding uses the row's own label in computing its encoding, causing target leakage (the model sees a feature computed from the answer). CatBoost imposes a random permutation of the rows and encodes each row using only the labels of rows earlier in that permutation, e.g. ∑prior rows with level cy+a pcount+a\frac{\sum_{\text{prior rows with level }c}y+a\,p}{\text{count}+a}count+a∑prior rows with level c​y+ap​ with prior ppp and weight aaa. Ordered boosting extends this idea to the gradient computation itself to reduce prediction shift (the bias from computing residuals on the same rows used to fit the model). Symmetric trees act as a regularizer and make inference fast.

#Key hyperparameters and tuning order

Hyperparameter Effect Direction when overfitting
learning_rate (ν\nuν) Step size per tree Lower (and raise rounds)
n_estimators / rounds Capacity of the ensemble Use early stopping
max_depth / num_leaves Interaction depth, tree complexity Lower
min_child_weight / min_data_in_leaf Minimum evidence per leaf Raise
subsample (rows), colsample_* (features) Randomization and variance reduction Lower (for example toward 0.6 to 0.9)
lambda (L2), alpha (L1), gamma / min_gain_to_split Penalize leaf weights, complexity Raise

A sensible tuning order, from largest effect to finest:

  1. Fix a sensible objective and metric, and set a moderately high learning rate (about 0.05 to 0.1) with early stopping to find the right number of rounds quickly.
  2. Tune tree complexity: max_depth or num_leaves, then min_child_weight / min_data_in_leaf.
  3. Tune randomization: row and column subsampling.
  4. Tune regularization: λ\lambdaλ, α\alphaα, γ\gammaγ.
  5. Lower the learning rate (for example to 0.01 to 0.03), raise the number of rounds with early stopping, and refit.

Use random or Bayesian search rather than grids; the ranges above are heuristics that depend on the dataset size and noise level, so treat them as starting points. For imbalanced problems, choose the evaluation metric first (PR-AUC or log loss) and consider class weighting only if the ranking, not the calibrated probability, is the goal.

#Early stopping

Hold out a validation set (or use CV folds); at each round compute the validation metric and stop when it fails to improve for early_stopping_rounds rounds, then keep the best iteration. Details that matter:

  • The validation set is used for model selection, so its score is optimistically biased; report final performance on a separate test set (or nested validation).
  • The split must mirror deployment: time-based for temporal data, group-based when rows cluster by entity, otherwise leakage makes early stopping choose too many rounds.
  • In CV, per-fold best iterations differ; use the mean or median when refitting on all data, perhaps with a small increase for the larger training set.
  • A noisy validation metric on small data can stop prematurely; use a patient early_stopping_rounds and a low learning rate.

#Interpreting GBDT: SHAP and permutation importance

SHAP assigns each feature a Shapley value: its average marginal contribution to the prediction over all orderings of features, which satisfies local accuracy (the contributions plus a base value sum to the prediction), missingness, and consistency. TreeSHAP computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in polynomial time, in contrast to the exponential cost of the brute-force definition. Practical points:

  • Local (per-prediction) explanations aggregate into global ones (mean absolute SHAP), and dependence plots show effect shape and interactions.
  • "Missing" features must be defined: the interventional version breaks dependence using a background dataset, the path-dependent version follows the trees' own cover statistics. With correlated features the two can differ: the path-dependent version can assign nonzero credit to a feature the model never splits on, while the interventional version evaluates the model on off-manifold combinations; state which one is used.
  • SHAP explains the model's behavior, not causal effects, and is for the model's output scale (log-odds for classifiers), which may differ from probability scale.

Permutation importance (on held-out data) measures performance dependence directly and is model-agnostic; use it to sanity-check SHAP or gain-based rankings, and group correlated features when permuting.

#When GBDT beats deep nets on tabular data (hedged)

Gradient-boosted trees are commonly the strongest or near-strongest family on medium-sized, heterogeneous tabular data (a few thousand to a few hundred thousand rows with mixed numeric/categorical features), and published benchmark comparisons have found tuned tree ensembles competitive with or better than standard deep architectures in that regime, with much less tuning. Plausible reasons, which are explanatory hypotheses rather than settled laws:

  • Tabular targets are often irregular and non-smooth in the features, and trees represent sharp, axis-aligned thresholds naturally while smooth networks have a bias toward low-frequency functions.
  • Uninformative features are common; trees' split selection is comparatively robust to them, while networks use all inputs.
  • Features have heterogeneous meaning and scale (no spatial or sequential structure to exploit), the case where convolution or attention priors give little.
  • Trees need little preprocessing (no scaling, native handling of missing values), are fast to train, and tune relatively easily.

Conditions that can shift the balance toward neural networks: very large datasets; multimodal inputs (text, image, sequences) feeding the tabular model; the need for end-to-end learned embeddings of high-cardinality entities; multi-task or transfer settings; or very small datasets where pretrained tabular models may help. Ensembling a GBDT with a network often beats either alone. The honest interview position is: start with a regularized linear baseline and a tuned GBDT, and move to deep models when data type, scale, or the need for learned representations justifies the cost; verify with a proper validation scheme on your data rather than relying on a benchmark.

Interview angle

A strong hedged answer to "Why not just use a neural network?": On mid-sized tabular data with mixed types, tuned GBDTs are often as good or better, train faster, and need little preprocessing; benchmark both anyway, and consider a network if there is a lot of data, unstructured inputs, or a need for learned embeddings.

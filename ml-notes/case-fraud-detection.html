Case Study: Fraud and Abuse Detection

Fraud detection is a classification problem where positives are rare, labels arrive late and are partly missing, the adversary adapts to your model, and the right threshold is a business cost, not a statistic.

This case study designs a real-time fraud and abuse system for a payments and marketplace platform: every transaction is scored before it is approved, and a smaller set of account-level events (signups, logins, payouts) are scored for abuse. The same architecture, with different features, serves other abuse problems such as fake accounts and promo exploitation. The core difficulties are the ones named in the framing line, and they interact: because the model blocks transactions, it also changes which labels it will ever see.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Scope a fraud problem with prevalence, volume, review capacity, and cost-of-error numbers, and verify the arithmetic.
  • Explain why a layered design (rules, model, graph and review) beats a single model.
  • Handle extreme class imbalance without breaking calibration, and handle delayed and selection-biased labels with time-based splits.
  • Choose a decision threshold from costs and capacity, and report precision at a fixed review capacity.
  • Describe graph features, adversarial adaptation, drift monitoring, and active learning for the review queue.
  • Discuss explainability and the cost of false positives to legitimate customers.

#Problem statement and clarifying questions

Prompt. Design a system that detects fraudulent transactions on a platform processing around 200 million transactions per day, deciding in real time whether to approve, challenge, hold, or decline each one.

#Requirements and back-of-envelope scale

Traffic. 200×106/86,400≈2,315200\times10^6 / 86{,}400 \approx 2{,}315200×106/86,400≈2,315 transactions per second on average. With a peak factor of 4 (evening and event spikes), plan for about 9,2609{,}2609,260 per second.

Fraud volume. At 0.1 percent prevalence, 200×106×0.001=200,000200\times10^6 \times 0.001 = 200{,}000200×106×0.001=200,000 fraudulent transactions per day. A classifier that approves everything is 99.9 percent accurate, which is why accuracy is useless here (Class Imbalance).

Review capacity. 400×100=40,000400 \times 100 = 40{,}000400×100=40,000 cases per day, which is 40,000/(200×106)=0.02%40{,}000 / (200\times10^6) = 0.02\%40,000/(200×106)=0.02% of transactions. If analysts were the only defense, even perfect precision would catch at most 40,000/200,000=20%40{,}000/200{,}000 = 20\%40,000/200,000=20% of fraud; at 60 percent precision, 24,00024{,}00024,000 confirmed cases, or 12 percent of the day's fraud. The consequence is the central design principle: automation must handle the bulk, and humans are reserved for the ambiguous middle.

Feature lookups. If each decision needs around 40 online features, peak lookups are 9,260×40≈370,0009{,}260 \times 40 \approx 370{,}0009,260×40≈370,000 reads per second, which dictates a low-latency key-value store with precomputed aggregates rather than on-the-fly warehouse queries.

Training data size. Over a 90-day window there are 90×200,000=18M90 \times 200{,}000 = 18\text{M}90×200,000=18M positives and about 17.98B17.98\text{B}17.98B negatives. Keeping all positives and 1 percent of negatives gives roughly 18M+180M≈198M18\text{M} + 180\text{M} \approx 198\text{M}18M+180M≈198M rows, a size that trains comfortably on a cluster, at the price of a calibration correction (below).

Decision bands. Scores are mapped to actions, not to a single approve or decline. Low scores are approved; high scores are declined or blocked; the middle band triggers step-up authentication (for example a one-time code), a hold, or review. Step-up is valuable because it converts a probabilistic decision into evidence, and it generates labels cheaply.

#Layering: rules, model, graph, and humans

A single model is rarely the right design. Each layer has a distinct job:

Layer Role Strength Weakness
Rules Hard policy (sanctions lists, known-bad devices, velocity caps) and fast emergency response Instant to deploy, transparent, no training lag Brittle, accumulate, hard to retire
Model Learns interactions across hundreds of signals, produces a calibrated risk score Generalizes beyond known patterns Label lag, needs retraining, opaque
Graph Detects rings through shared devices, cards, addresses, payout accounts Catches coordinated behavior invisible per-transaction Computation cost, risk of guilt by association
Human review Resolves ambiguity, produces high-quality labels Judgment, context Scarce, slow, inconsistent

Rules and models are complementary in time: when a new attack appears, a rule can be live in minutes, while the model needs fresh labeled examples. A good practice is to treat every rule as a hypothesis with a measured precision and an owner, retire rules the model has absorbed, and feed rule outputs in as model features (with care to avoid the model simply copying a rule).

#Data and labels

Labels are the hardest part of this problem, and they are flawed in four distinct ways.

  1. Delay. A chargeback may arrive 30 to 60 days after the transaction. Recent data is therefore right-censored: a transaction from last week labeled "not fraud" may simply not have matured. Training on it injects false negatives. The fix is a maturation window: train only on transactions old enough that most fraud has surfaced (for example 45 days), and use faster proxy labels (analyst decisions, customer reports, step-up failures) for recent weeks, with their different noise characteristics recorded.
  2. Selection bias. The system only observes outcomes for what it approved. A declined fraudulent transaction yields no chargeback, and a declined good one yields no complaint that gets recorded. A model trained only on approved traffic learns a distorted distribution, and the bias tends to grow as the model's decisions shape which transactions are ever observed. Mitigations: a small random-approve holdout (approve some borderline transactions despite a high score, bounded by cost), reject inference techniques with their assumptions stated, and using labels from step-up flows and review.
  3. Noise. Chargebacks include "friendly fraud" (a genuine customer disputes a legitimate purchase), and analysts disagree. Keep label provenance as a column and consider weighting or separate heads.
  4. Incomplete coverage. Fraud that is never detected is labeled negative. Estimates of true prevalence from audits of random samples give a sanity check on the labeled rate.

Time-based splitting. Random splits leak: the same card, device, or ring appears in both train and test, and patterns from the future inform the past (Data Leakage). Split by time with a gap equal to the maturation window, and evaluate on the most recent matured period, as in Time Series Validation. For account-level abuse, also split by entity so a ring is entirely in train or entirely in test when measuring generalization to new rings.

Point-in-time features. Velocity counters and aggregates must be computed as of the decision time, not from a later snapshot; see ML in Production on point-in-time joins and Training-Serving Skew.

#Features

Family Examples
Transaction Amount, currency, merchant category, time of day, payment method, shipping versus billing mismatch
Entity history Account age, historical spend mean and variance, past disputes, ratio of amount to account norm
Velocity Count and sum of attempts per card, device, IP, account in the last 1 minute, 1 hour, 24 hours, 7 days
Device and network Device fingerprint age, emulator or proxy signals, IP reputation, geolocation distance from last event
Behavioral Session telemetry, typing and navigation patterns, time from signup to first high-value action
Graph Degree and connected-component size of shared identifiers, fraction of neighbors flagged, embeddings from a graph model
Context Merchant risk score, event or promotion periods

Streaming aggregates are central: velocity features computed in a stream processor and written to the online store at second-level freshness. Ratios against the entity's own history (for example the amount as a multiple of the 30-day median) are usually more informative than raw values because they normalize between small and large spenders.

Graph features. Fraud rings reuse infrastructure: several accounts share a device, a payout account, or a shipping address. Useful graph signals are connected-component size and age, the number of distinct accounts per device or card, the proportion of neighbors with confirmed fraud (label propagation), and learned node embeddings from a graph neural network (Graph Neural Networks). Two cautions: neighbor-label features leak if the neighbors' labels were determined after the decision time, so compute them point in time; and shared infrastructure can be innocent (a household, a university network), so guilt by association needs thresholds and secondary evidence. Heavy graph computation usually runs near-real-time on a graph store and publishes compact features to the online store rather than traversing the graph inside the 100 ms path.

#Model choices

Option Fit Trade-off
Logistic regression Baseline, easy reason codes Limited interactions
Gradient boosted trees Strong default on tabular, fast inference, handles missing values and mixed scale Needs feature engineering; interactions are tree-limited
Deep models (sequence models over event history, entity embeddings) Learn from raw behavior sequences More data and serving cost, harder to explain
Graph neural network Captures ring structure Heavier infrastructure, staleness
Anomaly detection (isolation forest, autoencoder) Unsupervised signal for new attack types High false positive rate; best as a feature or alert, not the sole gate

A reasonable stack: gradient boosted trees (Gradient Boosting) as the primary scorer, with graph-derived and sequence-derived features as inputs and an unsupervised anomaly score as an additional input. Multiple models can specialize by segment (card-present versus online, new versus established accounts) when the base rates and attack types differ, at the cost of more models to maintain.

Imbalance handling. Options, in order of how often they are enough: (a) no resampling, with class weights or a metric suited to imbalance; (b) downsampling negatives, which cuts training cost but distorts probabilities; (c) oversampling or synthetic positives, which risks overfitting to duplicated points and is often unnecessary with boosted trees. If negatives are kept with probability β\betaβ, scores must be corrected to recover the true probability:

p=β psβ ps−ps+1p = \frac{\beta\, p_s}{\beta\, p_s - p_s + 1}p=βps​−ps​+1βps​​

where psp_sps​ is the model's output on the sampled data. With β=0.01\beta = 0.01β=0.01, a sampled score of 0.50.50.5 corresponds to a true probability of 0.01⋅0.5/(0.005−0.5+1)≈0.00990.01 \cdot 0.5 / (0.005 - 0.5 + 1) \approx 0.00990.01⋅0.5/(0.005−0.5+1)≈0.0099, about 1 percent, not 50 percent. Skipping this step makes thresholds meaningless and breaks cost-based decisions; verify with a reliability check on an un-resampled validation window (Calibration).

Common mistake

Evaluating on a resampled, balanced validation set. Precision on a 50/50 set bears no relation to precision at 0.1 percent prevalence.

#Thresholding by business cost and capacity

Given a calibrated probability ppp of fraud, declining a transaction is better than approving it when the expected loss from fraud exceeds the expected loss from friction:

p⋅CFN>(1−p)⋅CFP  ⟹  p>CFPCFP+CFNp \cdot C_{FN} > (1 - p)\cdot C_{FP} \;\Longrightarrow\; p > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}p⋅CFN​>(1−p)⋅CFP​⟹p>CFP​+CFN​CFP​​

With CFP=5C_{FP}=5CFP​=5 and CFN=80C_{FN}=80CFN​=80, the threshold is 5/85≈0.0595/85 \approx 0.0595/85≈0.059. This is a statement about calibrated probabilities; with a miscalibrated model, the same score threshold encodes a different risk. In practice the costs vary by amount (a missed 5,000-unit fraud is not an 80-unit loss), so the threshold is per-transaction, using the amount as a multiplier on CFNC_{FN}CFN​.

Capacity constraint. The review queue limits how many borderline cases can be examined. Two operating modes:

  • Cost-optimal: apply the formula, then check that the middle band fits capacity.
  • Capacity-constrained: rank by expected value of review (risk times amount) and review the top NNN per day. The metric is then precision at capacity (precision among the top 40,000 daily cases) together with recall of fraud value captured.

Because the queue is small relative to the problem, scoring bands need an auto-resolve path (step-up authentication) to avoid sending everything uncertain to people. Thresholds are revisited whenever costs, fraud mix, or capacity change, and should be separable from model training so operations can adjust them quickly.

#Real-time versus batch

Path Latency Uses Examples
Real-time scoring Under 100 ms Decide at authorization with online features Transaction approve, challenge, decline
Near-real-time Seconds to minutes Stream aggregates, graph updates, account-level triggers Velocity counters, ring detection on new links
Batch Hours to days Deep behavior models, full-graph analysis, backfills, retrospective review Nightly sweep of approved transactions, payout holds

Batch detection catches fraud the real-time path missed, enabling actions such as delaying a payout, freezing an account, or recalling a shipment. It also serves as a second chance where the cost of waiting is low. Keep the feature definitions shared between paths to limit skew. Fail-open versus fail-closed behavior must be an explicit policy when the scoring service is unavailable (for example approve low-value transactions and hold high-value ones), with rules still applied as a safety net.

#Adversarial adaptation and drift

Fraudsters probe: they test small amounts, vary devices, and adapt to whatever gets blocked. This changes how the system must be run.

  • Concept drift is the default. The relationship between features and fraud shifts as tactics change, not only the input distribution; see Data Drift vs Concept Drift. Retrain frequently (daily or weekly for fast-moving segments) on matured labels plus fresh proxy labels, and keep a champion-challenger setup.
  • Avoid revealing the decision boundary. Generic decline messages, randomized step-up, and delayed decisions give attackers less information. Rate-limit probing.
  • Feature robustness. Prefer signals that are costly to fake (account tenure, long-lived device history, payment instrument age) over easily spoofed ones. Monitor feature importance shifts as an attack indicator.
  • Rapid response lane. Rules, deny lists, and per-segment threshold changes act within minutes while a retrained model takes longer.
  • Red teaming. Periodically simulate attacks against the staging system to find weak features.
  • Monitoring. Track score distribution, decline and challenge rates by segment, step-up pass rates, approval rate, chargeback rate by cohort once matured, and the precision of each rule. A sudden drop in the fraud rate may mean the attackers moved to a segment you do not measure, not that the problem is solved.

#Review queues and active learning

The review queue is both an operational process and the best source of high-quality labels. Design it deliberately:

  • Prioritization. Rank cases by expected loss avoided, risk times amount, not by score alone; break ties by case age to meet service levels.
  • Active learning. Reserve a portion of review capacity for uncertain cases (scores near the threshold) and for a random sample of lower scores. Uncertainty sampling improves the labels where the model is least certain, and the random sample gives unbiased performance estimates and covers blind spots. Mixing the two avoids a feedback loop where only the model's confident cases are ever examined.
  • Analyst tooling. Show the model's reason codes, linked entities from the graph, and prior decisions to speed decisions and improve consistency.
  • Quality control. Double-review a sample to measure analyst agreement, and track label turnaround so that fast labels can feed retraining.
  • Capacity feedback. If the middle band overflows capacity, tighten the band or add automated step-up; if it underflows, widen the band to harvest more labels.

#Evaluation

Offline.

  • Use PR-AUC and precision at a fixed recall or capacity, not accuracy or ROC-AUC alone; ROC-AUC can look excellent at extreme imbalance while precision is poor (ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC, Precision and Recall). At 0.1 percent prevalence a random scorer has precision 0.001, which is the baseline to compare against.
  • Evaluate value-weighted metrics: fraud dollars caught, false-positive dollars, and net savings under the cost model, in addition to counts.
  • Evaluate on a time-based hold-out with a maturation gap, reported per segment (new accounts, payment types, geographies).
  • Check calibration by reliability curve and expected calibration error (Calibration), since thresholds depend on it.
  • Run backtests: replay the last several weeks day by day with the deployed policy and the candidate policy, applying the capacity constraint, to estimate net savings and queue load.

Online.

  • A/B tests are possible but slow because the main outcome (chargebacks) is delayed and rare; use leading indicators (decline and challenge rates, step-up pass rate, analyst confirmed-fraud rate in review) for early read and the matured chargeback rate for the final read (A/B Testing & Experimentation).
  • Randomize at the entity level (account or card) where possible, since a ring touching both arms contaminates the comparison.
  • Guardrails: approval rate, customer complaints, support contacts, conversion on challenged flows, and latency.
  • Shadow mode for a new model: score without acting, compare with the incumbent and with later labels, then ramp.

#Explainability and false-positive cost

Declines have costs beyond the 5-unit placeholder: lost customer lifetime value, support load, and trust. Good customers who are wrongly declined may not return, so the cost can be skewed toward high-value, high-frequency customers. Design implications:

  • Track false positive rate by customer segment, including a fairness check so that the model does not systematically burden certain regions or groups; see the fairness checks in ML in Production.
  • Provide reason codes for each decision (top contributing features through SHAP-style attributions or a surrogate), logged for audit and shown to analysts. Tree models make this practical at serving cost. Reason codes shown to customers should be generic enough not to teach attackers.
  • Prefer step-up over decline in the gray zone, since a customer who passes a challenge is retained and a fraudster often fails.
  • Provide an appeal path and use appeals as labels.
  • Keep a decision log (inputs, score, rule hits, model version, action) for audit and reproducibility.
Interview angle

Weak answers say "use SMOTE and optimize F1". Strong answers state the cost matrix, derive the threshold from calibrated probabilities, name the label delay and selection bias, and mention step-up authentication and review capacity as part of the decision policy.

#Failure modes

Failure Symptom Mitigation
Label delay misread as improvement Recent fraud rate looks low Maturation window, cohort-based reporting
Selection bias Model degrades as it improves; blind spot where it declines Random-approve holdout, step-up labels, review sampling
Uncalibrated scores after downsampling Thresholds trigger too many or too few cases Prior correction, reliability check on natural distribution
Leakage via random splits or future aggregates Offline AUC unrealistically high Time-based splits with gap, point-in-time features
Adversarial adaptation Sudden spike in a segment, rule precision decays Rapid-response rules, frequent retraining, red teaming
Guilt by association Innocent households or networks blocked Graph thresholds, corroborating evidence, appeals
Queue overflow Slow decisions, stale labels Capacity-aware bands, auto-resolve via step-up
Skew between train and serve Production precision below backtest Shared feature code, skew monitoring
Service outage No scoring Defined fail-open or fail-closed policy by value, rules as fallback
Rule accumulation Conflicting, unexplained rules Owners, precision tracking, scheduled review and retirement

Causal Inference for ML Engineers

A predictive model answers "what will happen?"; a causal model answers "what happens if we do this?" The two questions need different tools.

Most production ML is trained to predict: click, churn, default, purchase. Many business decisions, though, are interventions: send the coupon, show the ranking, change the price. A model that predicts well under the current policy can be useless, or harmful, for choosing the action, because the correlations it exploits were produced by the old policy and by hidden drivers of both action and outcome. This chapter covers the vocabulary (potential outcomes), why naive comparisons fail, and the toolbox for estimating effects when a randomized experiment is possible, partially possible, or not possible at all. For the mechanics of designing and analysing randomized tests, see A/B Testing & Online Experimentation; this chapter starts where randomization stops being available.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Define potential outcomes and distinguish ATE, ATT, and CATE, and explain the fundamental problem of causal inference.
  • Explain why correlation fails (confounding, selection bias) and why randomization fixes it.
  • Estimate an effect with propensity scores (matching, IPW, doubly robust) and state the assumptions that make it valid.
  • Choose between difference-in-differences, synthetic control, instrumental variables, and regression discontinuity for a given setup.
  • Build and evaluate an uplift model, including Qini curves, and name the S/T/X-learner trade-offs.
  • Read a causal DAG and tell a confounder from a mediator from a collider.

#Potential outcomes and the estimands

For each unit iii and a binary treatment Ti∈{0,1}T_i \in \{0,1\}Ti​∈{0,1}, define two potential outcomes: Yi(1)Y_i(1)Yi​(1), the outcome if treated, and Yi(0)Y_i(0)Yi​(0), the outcome if not. The individual causal effect is Yi(1)−Yi(0)Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)Yi​(1)−Yi​(0). Only one of the two is ever observed:

Yi=Ti Yi(1)+(1−Ti) Yi(0)Y_i = T_i\,Y_i(1) + (1 - T_i)\,Y_i(0)Yi​=Ti​Yi​(1)+(1−Ti​)Yi​(0)

This is the fundamental problem of causal inference: the counterfactual is missing for every unit, so individual effects are never directly observed. Everything that follows is a strategy for replacing the missing counterfactual with something credible, usually a comparison group.

Three common targets, which differ in whom they average over:

Estimand Definition Question it answers
ATE E[Y(1)−Y(0)]\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]E[Y(1)−Y(0)] Effect if we treated everyone vs no one
ATT E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣T=1]\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T=1]E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣T=1] Effect on those who actually got treated
CATE E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x]\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X=x]E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x] Effect for units with covariates xxx (heterogeneity)

ATE and ATT coincide in a randomized trial with full compliance, but differ in observational data whenever the treated are unlike the population. CATE is the quantity that uplift models try to estimate, because targeting needs per-segment effects.

Two standing assumptions underlie most methods:

  • SUTVA (no interference, consistent treatment): one unit's outcome does not depend on others' treatment, and "treated" means one well-defined thing. Marketplaces, social networks, and shared-capacity systems violate the first part; see the switchback and cluster designs in the A/B chapter.
  • Consistency: the observed outcome of a treated unit equals its Y(1)Y(1)Y(1).
Memory hook

Potential outcomes: two lives per unit, only one lived. Causal inference is the art of borrowing the other life from someone similar.

#Confounding, selection bias, and why correlation fails

Suppose a retention-email campaign was sent mostly to already-engaged users. Treated users retain more, but they would have retained more anyway. A confounder is a variable that affects both treatment assignment and outcome, creating association that is not causal.

The naive difference in means decomposes as:

E[Y∣T=1]−E[Y∣T=0]⏟observed gap=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣T=1]⏟ATT+E[Y(0)∣T=1]−E[Y(0)∣T=0]⏟selection bias\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0]}_{\text{observed gap}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T=1]}_{\text{ATT}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}observed gapE[Y∣T=1]−E[Y∣T=0]​​=ATTE[Y(1)−Y(0)∣T=1]​​+selection biasE[Y(0)∣T=1]−E[Y(0)∣T=0]​​

The last term says: had nobody been treated, would the two groups still differ? If the treated would have done better anyway, the bias is positive and the naive gap overstates the effect. Selection bias is zero when treatment is independent of potential outcomes, which is exactly what randomization delivers.

Why a high-AUC model does not fix this. Adding "engagement" as a feature to a predictor of retention improves AUC, but the coefficient on "received email" is then an association adjusted for observed features only. Unobserved drivers (intent, hidden targeting rules, who the campaign manager chose) remain, and even with all confounders observed, a feature attribution or coefficient is not by itself a causal effect without an explicit causal design. A related ML-specific trap: training on logged data from a previous policy teaches the model that the previous policy's choices were good, which is selection bias in disguise. See also Data Leakage for the cousin problem of features that encode the outcome.

Common mistake

Controlling for everything available. Conditioning on a mediator removes part of the effect; conditioning on a collider creates bias out of nothing (see the DAG section). The adjustment set should come from a causal story, not from "all columns".

#Randomization

If TTT is assigned by a coin flip, then T⊥{Y(0),Y(1)}T \perp \{Y(0), Y(1)\}T⊥{Y(0),Y(1)}, so the treated and control groups are exchangeable and the difference in means is unbiased for the ATE. Randomization balances observed and unobserved covariates in expectation, which is why it is the gold standard. It does not guarantee balance in any single small sample, which is why stratified randomization and regression adjustment (including CUPED, covered in the A/B chapter) are used to reduce variance.

Randomization can still fail in practice:

  • Non-compliance: assigned is not the same as received. Analyse by assignment (intent-to-treat) and, if needed, use assignment as an instrument for receipt (see IV below).
  • Differential attrition: if dropout depends on treatment and outcome, balance is broken after the fact.
  • Interference: spillovers contaminate control.

When randomization is unavailable because of ethics, cost, or product constraints, the remaining sections are the fallback, each trading a stronger assumption for feasibility.

#Propensity scores

The propensity score is e(x)=P(T=1∣X=x)e(x) = P(T=1 \mid X=x)e(x)=P(T=1∣X=x). It rests on two assumptions:

  1. Unconfoundedness (ignorability): {Y(0),Y(1)}⊥T∣X\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \perp T \mid X{Y(0),Y(1)}⊥T∣X. Given the observed covariates, treatment is as good as random. This is untestable.
  2. Overlap (positivity): 0<e(x)<10 < e(x) < 10<e(x)<1 for all xxx in the support. Every kind of user has some chance of both arms.

Rosenbaum and Rubin showed that conditioning on the scalar e(X)e(X)e(X) is enough to balance all of XXX, which reduces a high-dimensional matching problem to one dimension. In practice e(x)e(x)e(x) is estimated with a classifier such as logistic regression or gradient boosting. Unlike a normal classifier, the goal is covariate balance, not accuracy, so check balance (standardized mean differences after weighting) rather than AUC.

#Matching and stratification

Matching pairs each treated unit with one or more control units that have similar propensity (or covariates) and compares outcomes within pairs. It estimates the ATT naturally and makes the comparison transparent, but discards unmatched units and needs care with caliper width. Stratification groups units into propensity bins (often five) and averages within-bin effects; it is coarse but easy to audit.

#Inverse propensity weighting (IPW)

IPW reweights each unit by the inverse of the probability of the arm it landed in, creating a pseudo-population in which treatment is independent of XXX:

τ^IPW=1n∑i=1n[TiYie(Xi)−(1−Ti) Yi1−e(Xi)]\hat\tau_{\text{IPW}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{T_i Y_i}{e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)\,Y_i}{1-e(X_i)}\right]τ^IPW​=n1​i=1∑n​[e(Xi​)Ti​Yi​​−1−e(Xi​)(1−Ti​)Yi​​]

Here e(Xi)e(X_i)e(Xi​) is the propensity score, TiT_iTi​ the treatment indicator, and YiY_iYi​ the observed outcome. A treated unit with e=0.2e = 0.2e=0.2 was unlikely to be treated, so it stands in for 1/0.2=51/0.2 = 51/0.2=5 units of its type. The formula targets the ATE (weights T/e+(1−T)/(1−e)T/e + (1-T)/(1-e)T/e+(1−T)/(1−e)); to target the ATT, use weights T+(1−T) e/(1−e)T + (1-T)\,e/(1-e)T+(1−T)e/(1−e), which keep treated units at weight 1 and reweight controls to look like the treated.

A campaign targets 1,000 users in two engagement segments. Treatment was not random: high-engagement users were treated more often.

Go deeper (optional)

IPW variance blows up when eee is near 0 or 1: one unit with e=0.01e = 0.01e=0.01 carries weight 100. #Doubly robust estimation (AIPW)

Outcome regression fits μt(x)=E[Y∣T=t,X=x]\mu_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=t, X=x]μt​(x)=E[Y∣T=t,X=x] and averages μ^1−μ^0\hat\mu_1 - \hat\mu_0μ^​1​−μ^​0​. IPW models the assignment. The augmented IPW estimator combines them:

τ^DR=1n∑i[μ^1(Xi)−μ^0(Xi)+Ti (Yi−μ^1(Xi))e(Xi)−(1−Ti) (Yi−μ^0(Xi))1−e(Xi)]\hat\tau_{\text{DR}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left[\hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i) + \frac{T_i\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_1(X_i))}{e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i))}{1 - e(X_i)}\right]τ^DR​=n1​i∑​[μ^​1​(Xi​)−μ^​0​(Xi​)+e(Xi​)Ti​(Yi​−μ^​1​(Xi​))​−1−e(Xi​)(1−Ti​)(Yi​−μ^​0​(Xi​))​]

It is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correctly specified, hence "doubly robust". With cross-fitting (estimate nuisances on held-out folds), it also tolerates flexible ML nuisance models, which is the idea behind double machine learning. Doubly robust does not rescue you from unmeasured confounding or lack of overlap.

Interview angle

Weak: "Match on features until the groups look similar." Strong: "Propensity methods assume no unmeasured confounding and overlap; check overlap with propensity histograms per arm, check balance after weighting, trim extreme weights, and run a sensitivity analysis for how strong an unobserved confounder would need to be to erase the effect."

Rapid fire: propensity methods
  • Propensity score =P(T=1∣X)= P(T=1 \mid X)=P(T=1∣X); its job is balance, not prediction accuracy.
  • Unconfoundedness is untestable; overlap is checkable with histograms.
  • IPW reweights to a pseudo-population where T⊥XT \perp XT⊥X.
  • Extreme weights inflate variance; clip, normalize, or trim.
  • AIPW is consistent if either the outcome or propensity model is right.
  • Matching targets ATT naturally; IPW targets ATE by default.
  • Sensitivity analysis (for example E-values or Rosenbaum bounds) quantifies how fragile the result is to hidden confounding.

#Difference-in-differences

When a policy hits one group at a known time and you observe both groups before and after, difference-in-differences (DiD) compares the change in the treated group with the change in a control group. The control group's change stands in for what would have happened to the treated group without the intervention.

Under the parallel trends assumption, absent treatment the treated and control groups' outcomes would have moved by the same amount:

τ^DiD=(Yˉtreat,post−Yˉtreat,pre)−(Yˉctrl,post−Yˉctrl,pre)\hat\tau_{\text{DiD}} = \left(\bar Y_{\text{treat,post}} - \bar Y_{\text{treat,pre}}\right) - \left(\bar Y_{\text{ctrl,post}} - \bar Y_{\text{ctrl,pre}}\right)τ^DiD​=(Yˉtreat,post​−Yˉtreat,pre​)−(Yˉctrl,post​−Yˉctrl,pre​)

Level differences between groups are allowed and are differenced out; only differing trends break the estimate.

#Worked example: a 2x2 DiD

A feature launches in one region. Weekly conversion rate (percent):

Group Before After Change
Treated region 10.0 14.0 +4.0
Control region 9.0 11.0 +2.0

A naive before/after on the treated region says +4.0+4.0+4.0 points, but the control region also rose 2.02.02.0 points for reasons unrelated to the feature (seasonality, a market-wide promotion). DiD =4.0−2.0=2.0= 4.0 - 2.0 = 2.0=4.0−2.0=2.0 points. Equivalently, the post-period gap (3.0) minus the pre-period gap (1.0) is 2.0.

Checking and strengthening the assumption. Parallel trends is about the counterfactual, so it cannot be tested directly, but pre-period data help: plot several pre-periods and check the lines move together, and run an event-study regression with leads and lags (leads should be near zero). Pitfalls:

  • Anticipation effects (behavior changes before launch) contaminate the pre-period.
  • Regression to the mean if the treated group was chosen for an unusual recent value (Ashenfelter's dip).
  • Staggered rollouts with heterogeneous effects can bias the classic two-way fixed effects estimator; modern estimators handle this, and a careful answer mentions it.
  • Standard errors should be clustered at the level of treatment assignment, or inference is overconfident.

#Synthetic control

When there is one treated unit (a country, a city, a single large customer) and many potential controls, DiD with one hand-picked control is fragile. Synthetic control builds a weighted combination of control units, with non-negative weights summing to one, that matches the treated unit's pre-period trajectory as closely as possible. The post-period gap between the treated unit and its synthetic twin is the estimated effect.

w^=arg⁡min⁡w≥0, ∑jwj=1∑t≤T0(Y1t−∑jwjYjt)2\hat w = \arg\min_{w \ge 0,\ \sum_j w_j = 1} \sum_{t \le T_0}\Bigl(Y_{1t} - \sum_{j} w_j Y_{jt}\Bigr)^2w^=argw≥0, ∑j​wj​=1min​t≤T0​∑​(Y1t​−j∑​wj​Yjt​)2

Here Y1tY_{1t}Y1t​ is the treated unit's outcome at time ttt, YjtY_{jt}Yjt​ the donor units', and T0T_0T0​ the intervention time. Good pre-treatment fit is the credibility check: if the synthetic unit cannot track the treated unit before the launch, the post gap means little. Inference typically uses placebo tests, which re-run the method pretending each donor was treated and ask whether the real gap is unusually large. Limits: needs a long pre-period, donors must be unaffected by the intervention, and the treated unit must lie inside the convex hull of donors. It is the standard answer to "we launched in one market only and want the effect".

#Instrumental variables

When unmeasured confounding rules out adjustment, an instrument ZZZ can still identify an effect. ZZZ must satisfy:

  1. Relevance: ZZZ affects treatment TTT.
  2. Exclusion: ZZZ affects the outcome YYY only through TTT.
  3. Independence: ZZZ is as good as randomly assigned (unrelated to confounders).
  4. Monotonicity (needed to interpret the estimate as a LATE): no defiers, i.e. nobody takes treatment less because of the instrument.

With a binary instrument, the Wald estimator is the ratio of two effects:

τ^IV=E[Y∣Z=1]−E[Y∣Z=0]E[T∣Z=1]−E[T∣Z=0]\hat\tau_{\text{IV}} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z=0]}{\mathbb{E}[T \mid Z=1] - \mathbb{E}[T \mid Z=0]}τ^IV​=E[T∣Z=1]−E[T∣Z=0]E[Y∣Z=1]−E[Y∣Z=0]​

The numerator is the effect of the instrument on the outcome (reduced form); the denominator is its effect on treatment uptake (first stage). Dividing rescales the intent-to-treat effect by the compliance rate. Two-stage least squares generalizes this to covariates and continuous instruments.

The classic ML-product instance is a randomized encouragement: users are randomly nudged to adopt a feature, but adoption is voluntary. Random assignment is the instrument, adoption is the treatment. Under monotonicity the estimate is a local average treatment effect (LATE): the effect for compliers, those whose behavior the instrument changes, not necessarily for always-takers or never-takers.

Common mistake

Weak instruments. If the first stage is small, the ratio is unstable and biased toward the confounded estimate. Report the first-stage F-statistic; values below roughly 10 are a conventional warning sign. The exclusion restriction is also untestable, so the argument for it has to be made on domain grounds.

#Regression discontinuity

When treatment is assigned by a threshold on a continuous running variable (credit score above 650 gets the offer, age above a cutoff gets a service), units just above and just below the cutoff are nearly identical except for treatment. The jump in outcome at the cutoff estimates the local effect:

τRD=lim⁡x↓cE[Y∣X=x]−lim⁡x↑cE[Y∣X=x]\tau_{\text{RD}} = \lim_{x \downarrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x]τRD​=x↓clim​E[Y∣X=x]−x↑clim​E[Y∣X=x]

where ccc is the cutoff. In practice, fit local linear regressions on each side within a bandwidth and compare the intercepts at ccc. Key checks: no manipulation of the running variable (a density test for bunching just on one side), smoothness of covariates at the cutoff, and robustness to bandwidth. The estimate is local to the cutoff and may not extrapolate. Many ML systems create accidental discontinuities (a score threshold for manual review, a rank cutoff for a carousel slot), which makes RD a useful tool for evaluating deployed decision rules without an experiment.

#DAGs: confounder, mediator, collider

A causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) encodes assumptions about what causes what. Three basic structures decide whether to adjust for a variable ZZZ when studying T→YT \to YT→Y:

  subgraph Confounder
    C1[Z] --> T1[T]
    C1 --> Y1[Y]
    T1 --> Y1
  end
  subgraph Mediator
    T2[T] --> M2[Z]
    M2 --> Y2[Y]
  end
  subgraph Collider
    T3[T] --> K3[Z]
    Y3[Y] --> K3
  end
Structure Path Adjust for ZZZ? Why
Confounder (fork) T←Z→YT \leftarrow Z \rightarrow YT←Z→Y Yes Opens a non-causal backdoor path; conditioning closes it
Mediator (chain) T→Z→YT \rightarrow Z \rightarrow YT→Z→Y Not for the total effect Conditioning blocks part of the causal effect; adjust only to study the direct effect, which needs further assumptions (no unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding)
Collider T→Z←YT \rightarrow Z \leftarrow YT→Z←Y No The path is closed by default; conditioning on ZZZ opens it and induces spurious association

Collider bias in ML terms: if a dataset only includes users who were approved (approval depends on both applicant quality and an unobserved trait), analysing within the approved set creates a correlation between the two causes. Selecting on the outcome or a consequence of it is a collider-type error and also underlies several selection-bias failures in offline evaluation of ranking systems (compare leakage, a different problem).

The backdoor criterion gives the recipe: find a set of variables that blocks every non-causal path from TTT to YYY without containing descendants of TTT, and adjust for it. The DAG is an assumption, not a result; two analysts with different DAGs can reach different adjustment sets from identical data.

Memory hook

Fork: adjust. Chain: leave it alone (for the total effect). Collider: do not touch.

Rapid fire: quasi-experiments
  • DiD needs parallel trends; check pre-trends and cluster standard errors.
  • Synthetic control needs good pre-period fit and unaffected donors; infer with placebo tests.
  • IV needs relevance, exclusion, independence, and (for a LATE) monotonicity; the estimate is for compliers.
  • Weak first stage means a fragile IV estimate.
  • RD compares units at a threshold; it is local and needs no manipulation of the running variable.
  • A DAG is an assumption set; the adjustment set follows from it.

#Uplift modelling

A churn model ranks users by risk; an uplift model ranks them by incremental benefit from treatment, the CATE τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x]\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X=x]τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x]. The distinction matters because targeting the highest-risk users wastes money on sure things (would convert anyway) and lost causes (will not convert regardless), and may irritate sleeping dogs (treatment reduces conversion). Value lies in the persuadables.

Uplift needs randomized (or well-adjusted) data with treatment and control labels; without them, the model learns the old targeting policy, not the effect.

#Evaluating uplift: Qini and uplift curves

There is no per-user ground truth for the effect, so evaluation compares groups. Sort users by predicted uplift, then for each top-kkk fraction compute the incremental outcome of treated over control within that slice, scaled to the population. The uplift curve plots the cumulative incremental gain against the fraction targeted; the Qini curve is the closely related version that weights by arm sizes. Compare to a random-targeting diagonal; the area between them (Qini coefficient or AUUC) summarizes ranking quality. Always evaluate on held-out randomized data. Standard metrics such as AUC measure outcome prediction, not effect ranking, and are not a substitute.

Interview angle

Asked "how would you decide whom to send the discount to?", a strong answer separates predicting purchase from predicting incremental purchase, asks for randomized holdout data, proposes a T- or X-learner baseline, evaluates with a Qini curve on a held-out randomized set, and converts uplift to profit using the offer cost, since the optimal threshold is where marginal uplift value equals marginal cost.

#Observational study or experiment: a decision table

Situation Preferred approach Main assumption to defend
Can randomize individuals, no interference A/B test Correct randomization unit, no SRM
Randomize, but spillovers (marketplace, network) Cluster, geo, or switchback experiment Clusters isolate interference
Cannot randomize; rich covariates; broad overlap Propensity / doubly robust adjustment No unmeasured confounding, overlap
Policy changed for one group at a known time; control group exists Difference-in-differences Parallel trends
One treated unit or market, many donors Synthetic control Pre-period fit, unaffected donors
Treatment assigned by a score threshold Regression discontinuity No manipulation at the cutoff
Unmeasured confounding but a randomized nudge or external shock Instrumental variables Exclusion, strong first stage
Need per-user targeting Uplift model on randomized data Randomized training data, held-out Qini

Two rules of thumb: prefer the design with the weakest untestable assumption you can afford, and when the stakes justify it, triangulate with two methods that fail in different ways. Observational estimates are also useful for choosing what to test, with the experiment then confirming it.

#Common pitfalls

  • Treating predictive feature importance as causal. A feature that predicts the outcome need not cause it, and the sign may flip under intervention.
  • Post-treatment conditioning. Adjusting for variables measured after treatment (mediators, colliders) biases the estimate.
  • Ignoring overlap. Extrapolating effects to regions with no counterfactual units is model-driven guessing.
  • Survivorship and selection on outcome. Evaluating only on users who stayed, converted, or were approved.
  • Peeking at many specifications. Trying propensity models and subgroups until the effect appears is multiple testing; see p-values and power.
  • Spurious precision. Reporting a narrow confidence interval from IPW without bootstrapping the full pipeline, including the propensity fit.
  • Training on logged policy data and deploying a new policy. Feedback loops and off-policy bias; counterfactual evaluation (IPW on logged bandit data) or exploration is needed.
  • Confusing statistical and causal validity. A well-powered test of a confounded comparison is precisely wrong.
  • Averaging away heterogeneity. A flat ATE can hide positive and negative subgroup effects; check CATE or at least pre-specified segments.

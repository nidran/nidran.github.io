A-B Testing & Experimentation

Statistics & Experimentation: from a hypothesis to a defensible launch decision.

An A/B test is a randomized controlled experiment: units are randomly assigned to variants, and any systematic difference in outcomes is attributed to the variant. Randomization is what turns a correlation into a causal estimate. This chapter covers designing the experiment, sizing it, validating it, analyzing it with the right variance estimate, and handling the situations where the textbook design breaks (interference, long-term effects, ranking systems, no randomization at all). It builds on Probability & Statistics.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Choose the randomization unit, hypotheses, and primary and guardrail metrics for a given product question.
  • Compute a required sample size from a baseline, a minimum detectable effect, α\alphaα, and power.
  • Diagnose a sample ratio mismatch and explain why it invalidates the readout.
  • Explain why peeking inflates false positives and name valid sequential alternatives.
  • Describe novelty and primacy effects, network interference, and marketplace effects, with mitigations.
  • Reduce variance with CUPED, handle ratio metrics with the delta method, and look for heterogeneous effects safely.
  • Pick a quasi-experimental method (difference-in-differences, synthetic control) when randomization is impossible.
  • Explain why offline model metrics may not predict online results, and how interleaving helps for ranking.

#The causal logic

Each unit iii has two potential outcomes, Yi(1)Y_i(1)Yi​(1) under treatment and Yi(0)Y_i(0)Yi​(0) under control, and only one is observed. The average treatment effect is ATE=E[Y(1)−Y(0)]\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]ATE=E[Y(1)−Y(0)]. Under random assignment, the treated and control groups are exchangeable in expectation, so

ATE^=YˉT−YˉC\widehat{\text{ATE}} = \bar Y_{T} - \bar Y_{C}ATE=YˉT​−YˉC​

is unbiased for the ATE. The estimate relies on the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA): one unit's outcome does not depend on others' assignments (no interference), and each variant is a single well-defined treatment. Many hard experimentation problems are violations of SUTVA.

#Randomization unit

The randomization unit (user, device, session, request, cluster, geography, time block) must match how the treatment is experienced and how outcomes are analyzed.

Unit Good for Watch out for
User / account Persistent product changes, consistent experience across visits Cross-device identity gaps (a person on two devices can land in both arms)
Session / request Stateless backend changes, fast power Same user sees both variants, which can contaminate learning effects and violates independence of observations
Cluster / geography Interference within groups (social network, local market) Few clusters means low power and high variance; needs cluster-level analysis
Time block (switchback) Marketplace or supply-side changes where users cannot be split Carryover between blocks; needs washout and many blocks

Rule of analysis: the unit of analysis should match (or be aggregated to) the unit of randomization. If assignment is by user but the metric is computed per page view, page views from the same user are correlated, and a naive standard error is too small. Aggregate to the user level, use the delta method (below), or use clustered standard errors.

Assignment should be deterministic and sticky, usually a hash of the unit id with an experiment-specific salt, so a unit sees one variant consistently and different experiments are independently randomized.

#Hypotheses

State them before the test:

  • H0H_0H0​: the treatment has no effect on the primary metric (δ=0\delta = 0δ=0).
  • H1H_1H1​: it does (δ≠0\delta \ne 0δ=0, two-sided), or specifically improves it (δ>0\delta > 0δ>0, one-sided).

A one-sided test is justifiable only when a change in the other direction would lead to the same decision (for example, "ship only if better"), and that choice must be made in advance. Two-sided tests are the common default because a harmful effect is also informative.

Also specify the minimum detectable effect (MDE): the smallest effect worth acting on. It should come from business or product judgment about the cost of the change, not from what the available sample happens to detect.

#Metrics

Role Purpose Examples (generic)
Primary (success) The one metric the decision hinges on; one or very few, fixed in advance Conversion rate, retention, revenue per user
Secondary / diagnostic Explain mechanisms and help interpret the primary Click-through rate, funnel step rates, engagement depth
Guardrail Detect unacceptable harm even if the primary improves Latency, crash rate, error rate, unsubscribe rate, cost per request

A good primary metric is sensitive (moves when the treatment works), attributable to the change, and aligned with the long-term goal. Often a proxy (short-term metric) is used for a long-term outcome; the proxy should be validated against the long-term outcome from past experiments. Guardrails are usually tested with non-inferiority logic: the goal is to rule out harm larger than a tolerance, which differs from trying to detect a benefit.

Avoid choosing the primary metric after seeing results. With many metrics, some will cross p<0.05p<0.05p<0.05 by chance; see multiple testing in Probability & Statistics.

Interview angle

A strong opening for any experiment design question: "What decision will this inform? Who is the unit? What is the primary metric and the smallest change that would change our decision? What would we monitor as guardrails?" Naming the decision and the MDE before the statistics signals that the design is driven by the product question.

#Sample size and power

For a two-sided, two-sample test comparing means with equal group sizes, equal variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 per observation, and a true difference δ\deltaδ, the required sample size per arm is

n=2 σ2 (z1−α/2+z1−β)2δ2.n = \frac{2\,\sigma^2\,\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\bigr)^2}{\delta^2}.n=δ22σ2(z1−α/2​+z1−β​)2​.

n=(z1−α/2+z1−β)2 [p1(1−p1)+p2(1−p2)](p2−p1)2.n = \frac{\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\bigr)^2\,\bigl[p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)\bigr]}{(p_2 - p_1)^2}.n=(p2​−p1​)2(z1−α/2​+z1−β​)2[p1​(1−p1​)+p2​(1−p2​)]​.

The rule of thumb gives about 15,000 using σ2≈0.094\sigma^2 \approx 0.094σ2≈0.094, which is close.

What the formula says about the levers:

  • n∝1/δ2n \propto 1/\delta^2n∝1/δ2. Halving the MDE needs about four times the sample.
  • n∝σ2n \propto \sigma^2n∝σ2. Variance reduction (CUPED, below) is equivalent to getting more traffic.
  • Power and α\alphaα trade off: stricter α\alphaα or higher power both raise nnn.
  • A fixed relative MDE on a small baseline rate is expensive: the absolute δ\deltaδ shrinks with ppp while the variance p(1−p)p(1-p)p(1−p) falls more slowly, so nnn grows roughly like 1/p1/p1/p.
  • Duration is nnn divided by the eligible traffic per day, rounded up to whole weeks so every day-of-week pattern is represented equally. Experiments typically run at least one to two full weekly cycles.

Caveats: the formula assumes independent observations at the randomization unit, roughly normal sampling distributions of the means, and a fixed analysis time. Heavy-tailed metrics may need winsorization or more data; clustered designs inflate the required nnn by the design effect 1+(m−1)ρ1 + (m-1)\rho1+(m−1)ρ for clusters of average size mmm and intra-cluster correlation ρ\rhoρ.

Power is chosen, not discovered, after the fact

"Post hoc" or "observed" power computed from the realized effect is a function of the p-value and adds no new information. Decide the MDE and power at design time; afterwards, report the confidence interval, which shows which effect sizes remain plausible.

#Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)

If the design allocates 50/50, the observed counts should be close to equal. SRM is a statistically significant deviation from the intended split. Test it with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test on the counts of units per arm:

χ2=∑k(Ok−Ek)2Ek,df=(number of arms)−1.\chi^2 = \sum_{k} \frac{(O_k - E_k)^2}{E_k}, \quad \text{df} = (\text{number of arms}) - 1.χ2=k∑​Ek​(Ok​−Ek​)2​,df=(number of arms)−1.

Example: 10,000 expected per arm but observed 10,300 vs 9,700 gives χ2=3002/10,000+3002/10,000=18\chi^2 = 300^2/10{,}000 + 300^2/10{,}000 = 18χ2=3002/10,000+3002/10,000=18, so p≈2×10−5p \approx 2\times10^{-5}p≈2×10−5. Teams commonly use a stricter alarm threshold (for example p<0.001p<0.001p<0.001) because the check runs on every experiment, and a false alarm is cheap relative to a missed one.

SRM indicates that the assignment or logging process is probably not the clean randomization assumed, so the treatment effect estimate cannot be trusted until the cause is understood, even when the shift looks small. Typical causes:

  • Variant-dependent filtering or bot handling (for example the treatment crashes some clients before logging).
  • Redirects or load-time differences causing differential loss of events.
  • Triggering conditions that differ by arm, or a bug in the assignment hash.
  • Cross-experiment interaction or a ramp-up that changed allocation mid-test.

The remedy is to find and fix the cause and rerun, not to analyze the data as is.

#Other checks

  • A/A test: run two identical variants; the false positive rate at α=0.05\alpha=0.05α=0.05 should be about 5% across many A/A runs. A significant departure suggests faulty variance estimates (e.g. ignoring clustering) or assignment problems.
  • Pre-experiment balance: covariates measured before assignment should not differ systematically between arms. A few imbalances among many covariates are expected by chance.
  • Instrumentation and logging parity: both arms should log the same events in the same way.
  • Triggered analysis: when only a subset of units would ever encounter the change, analyzing only units that triggered it (identified symmetrically in both arms) raises sensitivity, as untouched units add only noise. Trigger on the counterfactual-visible condition for both arms, not on an outcome.

#Peeking and sequential testing

A fixed-horizon test controls Type I error only if the analysis occurs once, at the pre-planned sample size. Checking the p-value repeatedly and stopping at the first p<0.05p<0.05p<0.05 turns the test into repeated trials, since the test statistic is a random walk that can cross the boundary at any look even when H0H_0H0​ is true. With about five equally spaced looks at nominal α=0.05\alpha=0.05α=0.05, the overall false positive rate is roughly 14%, and it keeps rising with more looks.

Valid ways to monitor an experiment:

Approach Idea Notes
Fixed horizon, no peeking Decide nnn up front; analyze once Simple and efficient; monitor only guardrails and SRM/health meanwhile
Group sequential designs Pre-specified number of interim looks with adjusted boundaries (alpha spending; O'Brien-Fleming is conservative early and spends little α\alphaα initially, Pocock uses roughly constant thresholds) Requires a planned look schedule; small cost in final-look power
Always-valid inference Sequential probability ratio tests (mSPRT) or confidence sequences give p-values/intervals valid at any stopping time Allows continuous monitoring; typically wider intervals than a fixed-horizon test at the same nnn
Bayesian monitoring Posterior probability or expected loss Stopping rules still affect frequentist error rates; bounds on error need to be checked with simulation, and results depend on the prior

Also, stopping early on a "significant" result biases the effect estimate upward (a form of the winner's curse), because the experiment stops when noise has pushed the estimate above the boundary.

Interview angle

When asked "can we stop early since it's already significant?", say that the nominal p-value no longer has its stated meaning after repeated looks; then offer the options: commit to a horizon, use alpha spending, or use always-valid methods. Mention that guardrail monitoring for severe harm is legitimate and often has its own stopping rule.

#Novelty, primacy, and time effects

  • Novelty effect: users react strongly to something new (clicking a redesigned button), and the lift decays as the novelty fades. Short tests overestimate the long-run effect.
  • Primacy / learning effect: users accustomed to the old experience initially perform worse with a better design and take time to adapt. Short tests underestimate the long-run effect.
  • Seasonality and day-of-week effects: whole-week durations and avoiding atypical periods (holidays, promotions) limit these.

Diagnostics: plot the treatment effect (difference in daily or weekly cohort means) over time since each unit's first exposure; a trend signals a transient effect. Compare effects for new users (no habit to unlearn, no novelty from change) against existing users. If the effect stabilizes, report the stabilized portion; if it does not, extend the test or use a long-term holdout (below).

#Interference: network and marketplace effects

SUTVA fails when treating one unit affects others' outcomes.

  • Social networks: if a user's treated friends change what the user sees or does, control users are indirectly treated. The naive estimate is typically biased toward zero for "network-effect" features (it understates the total effect), though the direction depends on the mechanism.
  • Two-sided marketplaces: treatment on one side shifts shared, finite resources. If a ranking change causes treated buyers to win more of a limited supply, control buyers get less, so the measured gap can overstate what a full launch would produce (the bias direction depends on whether the variants compete for or share the resource). Total effect is not the sum of per-user effects when resources are shared.
  • Shared infrastructure and budgets: ad budgets, inventory, driver supply, and caches create similar couplings.
Mitigation Idea Trade-off
Cluster randomization Randomize connected groups (graph clusters, regions) so most interactions stay within an arm Fewer effective units, lower power; cluster quality matters
Switchback (time-based) Alternate the whole system between variants over time blocks Carryover effects; needs many blocks and a washout
Geo experiments Randomize markets Few units, large variance; often paired with synthetic control for analysis
Budget- or supply-split designs Partition the shared resource between arms Complex; only models part of the interaction
Modeling interference Exposure mappings, simulations, or two-sided designs that estimate spillover explicitly Relies on assumptions about the interference structure

State the estimand you want: the effect of treating a subset (what the A/B measures under interference) is different from the effect of treating everyone (what a launch delivers).

#Variance reduction: CUPED

Variance sets sample size, so reducing it shortens experiments. CUPED (controlled-experiment using pre-experiment data) subtracts the part of the outcome predictable from a pre-treatment covariate XXX (for example the same metric measured before the experiment):

Yicuped=Yi−θ (Xi−Xˉ),θ=Cov(Y,X)Var(X).Y^{\text{cuped}}_i = Y_i - \theta\,(X_i - \bar X), \qquad \theta = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y, X)}{\text{Var}(X)}.Yicuped​=Yi​−θ(Xi​−Xˉ),θ=Var(X)Cov(Y,X)​.

Properties:

  • Because XXX is measured before assignment, it is independent of treatment, so the adjustment leaves the treatment effect estimate unbiased. A covariate affected by the treatment would bias it.
  • θ\thetaθ is estimated pooled across arms (or by regression), and Xˉ\bar XXˉ is the overall mean.
  • The variance of the adjusted outcome is

Var(Ycuped)=Var(Y) (1−ρ2),\text{Var}(Y^{\text{cuped}}) = \text{Var}(Y)\,(1-\rho^2),Var(Ycuped)=Var(Y)(1−ρ2),

where ρ\rhoρ is the correlation between YYY and XXX. A correlation of 0.7 cuts variance by about half, which is roughly halving the required sample size.

  • Equivalent to regressing YYY on treatment and XXX (ANCOVA); extending to several covariates or a machine learning prediction of YYY from pre-period features gives CUPAC-style variants.
  • It helps most for stable, persistent metrics with strong pre/post correlation; it helps little for new users or for metrics with no pre-period signal.

Related variance reducers: stratification / blocking (balance on important covariates at assignment), triggered analysis, winsorizing or capping extreme values (trades some bias for variance, so define the cap in advance and independently of arm), and choosing a more sensitive metric (for example a lower-variance proxy).

#Ratio metrics and the delta method

Many metrics are ratios of two per-user sums, such as click-through rate (total clicks / total impressions) or revenue per session. When randomization is by user but the ratio's denominator varies by user, the observations of the ratio are not i.i.d., and the usual formula s/ns/\sqrt{n}s/n​ on page-level rows is wrong because rows from the same user are correlated.

Let XiX_iXi​ and YiY_iYi​ be per-user numerator and denominator, with ratio R=Xˉ/YˉR = \bar X/\bar YR=Xˉ/Yˉ, and μx,μy\mu_x,\mu_yμx​,μy​ the means. The delta method (a first-order Taylor expansion) gives

Var ⁣(XˉYˉ)≈1n μy2[σx2−2R σxy+R2σy2],R=μxμy,\text{Var}\!\left(\frac{\bar X}{\bar Y}\right) \approx \frac{1}{n\,\mu_y^2}\Bigl[\sigma_x^2 - 2R\,\sigma_{xy} + R^2\sigma_y^2\Bigr], \qquad R = \frac{\mu_x}{\mu_y},Var(YˉXˉ​)≈nμy2​1​[σx2​−2Rσxy​+R2σy2​],R=μy​μx​​,

with all variances and the covariance computed across users. Use it to build a standard error for each arm's ratio, and for the difference between arms, add the arms' variances (independent groups).

Conditions: the approximation is good when nnn is large and Yˉ\bar YYˉ is not close to zero relative to its noise. Alternatives: bootstrap over users (resample users, not rows), or linearize the metric into a per-user quantity X~i=(Xi−R Yi)/μy\tilde X_i = (X_i - R\,Y_i)/\mu_yX~i​=(Xi​−RYi​)/μy​ and run an ordinary test on it.

Also note that the average of per-user ratios (mean of user-level CTRs) and the ratio of totals (global CTR) are different estimands: the first weights every user equally, the second weights users by their volume. Choose deliberately and state which one the metric is.

#Heterogeneous treatment effects

The ATE can hide differences across segments (new vs existing users, platforms, regions). The conditional effect is τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x]\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0) \mid X=x]τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x].

Practices:

  • Pre-register a short list of segments and test the treatment-by-segment interaction, rather than slicing post hoc. Examining many slices without correction produces false discoveries; apply FDR control to exploratory slices (see Probability & Statistics).
  • A significant overall effect with a significant segment effect does not mean the other segments are unaffected; compare the segments using an interaction test, not "significant here, non-significant there" (the difference between significant and not significant is not itself significant).
  • Subgroup samples are smaller, so effects are noisy and estimates for the most extreme segments tend to be inflated.
  • Causal ML methods (causal forests, meta-learners such as T-/X-/R-learners, uplift models) estimate τ(x)\tau(x)τ(x) for targeting. They need honest estimation (separate samples for structure and estimates) and validation, for example by comparing predicted to realized uplift by decile on held-out randomized data.

HTE matters when the decision is who gets the treatment (personalization, targeting), not just whether to ship.

#Long-term effects and holdouts

Short experiments measure short-run outcomes. Long-run outcomes can differ because of novelty, learning, ecosystem adaptation, or because the proxy metric is only loosely tied to the long-term goal.

  • Long-term holdout: keep a small randomized group (for example a few percent of traffic) on the old experience for weeks or months after launch, and compare cumulative outcomes. It costs some forgone benefit and adds maintenance burden when the holdout lags behind other changes.
  • Cumulative or global holdouts: hold out users from a whole bundle of launches in a period to measure the combined effect, which exposes interactions and diminishing returns that individual experiments cannot see.
  • Surrogate / proxy validation: use historical experiments to learn how short-term metrics relate to the long-term metric, and use the relationship (with uncertainty) to predict the long-term effect. Caveat: the relationship may not hold for new types of changes.
  • Cohort-based tracking: analyze users by exposure start date to separate time-since-exposure effects from calendar effects.

Optimizing a short-term metric can harm the long-term one (for example engagement tactics that raise clicks today but reduce satisfaction), so guardrails and long-term checks are part of the design.

#When an A/B test is not possible

Reasons: the treatment cannot be randomized (a policy change, a price law), ethical or legal limits, a tiny number of units, a one-time event, or a feature already launched everywhere. Observational methods can still estimate effects under explicit, untestable assumptions.

Method Setting Key assumption
Difference-in-differences (DiD) A treated group and an untreated group, each observed before and after the intervention Parallel trends: absent treatment, both groups' outcomes would have changed by the same amount
Synthetic control One (or a few) treated units, many potential comparison units with long pre-period data A weighted combination of control units reproduces the treated unit's pre-period trajectory and would have continued to track it
Regression discontinuity Treatment assigned by a threshold on a running variable Units just above and below the cutoff are comparable; no manipulation of the running variable
Instrumental variables Randomization or a natural source of variation that affects treatment but affects the outcome only through treatment Relevance and exclusion restriction (the latter is untestable); estimates a local effect for compliers
Matching / propensity scores / regression adjustment Treatment depends on observed covariates No unmeasured confounding (ignorability) and overlap between groups

The DiD estimator is

τ^DiD=(YˉT,post−YˉT,pre)−(YˉC,post−YˉC,pre).\hat\tau_{\text{DiD}} = \bigl(\bar Y_{T,\text{post}} - \bar Y_{T,\text{pre}}\bigr) - \bigl(\bar Y_{C,\text{post}} - \bar Y_{C,\text{pre}}\bigr).τ^DiD​=(YˉT,post​−YˉT,pre​)−(YˉC,post​−YˉC,pre​).

The first difference removes fixed differences between groups; the second removes common time shocks. Check parallel trends visually and with pre-period placebo tests (several pre-periods with no true effect), and cluster standard errors by the unit that is treated.

Synthetic control picks non-negative weights summing to one so that the weighted control units match the treated unit's pre-period outcomes; the post-period gap between the treated unit and its synthetic counterpart is the effect estimate. Inference uses placebo runs that apply the same procedure to untreated units.

Causal inference at a glance

The central obstacle is confounding: a variable that influences both who gets the treatment and the outcome. Randomization removes it by design; observational methods must argue it away with assumptions. Adjusting for a collider, or for a mediator (a variable affected by treatment) when the target is the total effect, can introduce bias, so adjustment sets should be justified with a causal diagram, not chosen by predictive power. Observational estimates are hypotheses for the experiments that become possible later.

Interview angle

When told "we cannot randomize", do not jump straight to a model. Ask what created the treatment group, what changed in the world at the same time, and what comparison group could stand in for the counterfactual. Then state the assumption (parallel trends, no unmeasured confounding) and how it could be probed with placebo or pre-trend checks.

#Offline vs online metric mismatch

A model that wins on offline metrics (AUC, NDCG, log loss on historical data) may not win online. Reasons:

  • Metric mismatch: the offline metric measures something other than the business outcome, for example AUC measures ranking over all pairs, while the product cares about the top few positions.
  • Feedback loops and selection bias: logged data come from the previous system's decisions, so labels exist only for what was shown (exposure bias). Evaluating a new policy on such data favors items the old system already liked unless corrected, for example with inverse propensity weighting or counterfactual estimators, which have high variance when propensities are small.
  • Distribution shift: offline data are historical, online traffic differs in time, seasonality, and composition.
  • Latency and system constraints: a more accurate but slower model can reduce engagement.
  • User behavior change: a new model changes what users do, which changes the data (the model is part of the environment).
  • Calibration and thresholds: ranking metrics can be unchanged while calibration shifts, affecting downstream decisions such as auctions or thresholds.
  • Winner's curse across many offline candidates: selecting the best of many noisy offline results overstates its true quality.

Practices: validate offline metrics against past online results (does an offline gain of size xxx historically translate to an online gain?), use time-based splits, evaluate on slices, run counterfactual evaluation where propensities are logged, and treat the A/B test as the final arbiter, with offline metrics as a screening filter. See Cross-Validation for offline evaluation setup.

#Interleaving for ranking

Comparing two rankers with a standard A/B test needs large samples because user-to-user variance is high and the metric (clicks) is noisy. Interleaving compares two rankers within the same user and query: results from rankers A and B are merged into one list, and clicks are credited to the ranker that contributed the clicked item. The ranker with more credited clicks over many queries wins.

Common merge schemes:

  • Team-draft interleaving: like picking teams, A and B alternate (randomizing who goes first each round) picking their highest-ranked item not already in the list; each item remembers its source.
  • Balanced and probabilistic variants: the latter sample rankers' scores to mix lists with controllable exposure.

Why it is attractive: each user is their own control, which removes between-user variance, so interleaving can detect preference differences with far less traffic than a between-user A/B test (commonly cited as an order-of-magnitude gain in sensitivity, depending on the setup).

Limits:

  • It yields a preference between two rankers, not an estimate of the effect size on a business metric such as revenue or retention.
  • The merged list is a different experience from either ranker alone, which matters for layouts where position or slate effects are strong.
  • It is awkward for rankers with very different result sets or when user behavior depends on the full page rather than item-level clicks.
  • It does not capture effects on long-term behavior.

A common pipeline: use offline metrics to filter candidates, interleaving to pick promising rankers quickly, and a full A/B test to measure business impact and guardrails before launch.

Rapid fire

Randomize where the treatment is felt, analyze where you randomized. Sample size scales as σ2/δ2\sigma^2/\delta^2σ2/δ2. SRM first, then results. No peeking without a sequential method. Guardrails use non-inferiority. CUPED needs a pre-period covariate. Ratio metrics need the delta method or user-level bootstrap. Marketplaces and networks violate no-interference; say which estimand the test targets. DiD hinges on parallel trends.

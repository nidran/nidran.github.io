Probability & Statistics

Statistics & Experimentation: the inference toolkit behind every metric, test, and model claim.

Most ML interview questions about "is this result real?", "how much data do we need?", or "why does this loss make sense?" reduce to probability and statistical inference. This chapter covers the rules of probability, the distributions that recur in ML, how estimators are judged, how likelihood-based fitting connects to regularization, and how confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are built and (mis)interpreted. The companion chapter A-B Testing & Experimentation applies all of this to online experiments.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Apply Bayes' theorem to a base-rate problem and explain why a positive test on a rare condition is usually a false alarm.
  • Match a distribution (Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, Normal, Exponential, Beta) to a modeling situation.
  • State the LLN and CLT with their conditions, and say what each does and does not guarantee.
  • Judge an estimator by bias, variance, and consistency, and relate MLE and MAP to loss functions and regularizers.
  • Interpret a confidence interval and a p-value correctly, and name the error types and power.
  • Pick a t-test, z-test, or chi-square test, and correct for multiple comparisons.

#Bayes' theorem

Rearranging the chain rule in two ways gives

P(H∣E)=P(E∣H) P(H)P(E)=P(E∣H) P(H)P(E∣H)P(H)+P(E∣Hc)P(Hc)P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)} = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E \mid H)P(H) + P(E \mid H^c)P(H^c)}P(H∣E)=P(E)P(E∣H)P(H)​=P(E∣H)P(H)+P(E∣Hc)P(Hc)P(E∣H)P(H)​

Read it as: posterior ∝\propto∝ likelihood ×\times× prior. The denominator P(E)P(E)P(E) is just the normalizer, computed with total probability.

#Worked base-rate example

A screening model flags a rare condition. Suppose:

  • Prevalence (prior): P(D)=0.01P(D) = 0.01P(D)=0.01.
  • Sensitivity (true positive rate): P(+∣D)=0.90P(+ \mid D) = 0.90P(+∣D)=0.90.
  • False positive rate: P(+∣Dc)=0.05P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.05P(+∣Dc)=0.05 (specificity 0.950.950.95).

Given a positive flag, the probability of the condition is

P(D∣+)=0.90×0.010.90×0.01+0.05×0.99=0.0090.009+0.0495=0.0090.0585≈0.154.P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.009}{0.009 + 0.0495} = \frac{0.009}{0.0585} \approx 0.154.P(D∣+)=0.90×0.01+0.05×0.990.90×0.01​=0.009+0.04950.009​=0.05850.009​≈0.154.

Only about 15% of flagged cases are true positives, despite 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity. A natural-frequency check with 10,000 people: 100 have the condition, 90 of them flag; 9,900 do not, and 495 of those flag. So 90/(90+495)≈15.4%90/(90+495) \approx 15.4\%90/(90+495)≈15.4%.

The result is driven by the prior. Precision depends on prevalence, which is why a classifier with fixed TPR/FPR has very different precision on a balanced test set than in production where positives are rare. This links directly to precision-recall trade-offs in imbalanced problems such as fraud or abuse detection.

Interview angle

When given a "99% accurate test" puzzle, state the three inputs (prior, sensitivity, false positive rate) before computing, then give the answer in natural frequencies. Mention that the conclusion changes with prevalence: the same model has higher precision in a high-prevalence slice. Interviewers often follow up with "what if the test is repeated?" Under conditional independence of repeated tests given the true state, the posterior odds multiply by the likelihood ratio 0.90/0.05=180.90/0.05 = 180.90/0.05=18 per positive result.

The odds form makes sequential updating clean: posterior odds=prior odds×likelihood ratio\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times \text{likelihood ratio}posterior odds=prior odds×likelihood ratio.

  • Memorylessness. The Exponential satisfies P(T>s+t∣T>s)=P(T>t)P(T > s+t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)P(T>s+t∣T>s)=P(T>t). It is the only continuous distribution with this property, which is why it implies a constant hazard rate. Real lifetimes often violate it.

Interview angle

A frequent follow-up: "what distribution would you use for X?" Answer by asking what is being counted or measured and what the generative story is. Conversion per session: Bernoulli. Conversions out of nnn sessions: Binomial. Support tickets per hour: Poisson, if arrivals are roughly independent and the rate is stable; check for overdispersion. Time until next event: Exponential, if hazard is roughly constant. Unknown rate with prior beliefs: Beta.

#Expectation, variance, covariance

Expectation is linear without any independence condition:

E[aX+bY+c]=a E[X]+b E[Y]+c.\mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] + c.E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c.

Variance measures spread:

Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−(E[X])2.\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2.Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−(E[X])2.

Covariance and correlation:

Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y],ρXY=Cov(X,Y)σXσY∈[−1,1].\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \rho_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} \in [-1,1].Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y],ρXY​=σX​σY​Cov(X,Y)​∈[−1,1].

Variance of a sum needs the covariance term:

Var(aX+bY)=a2Var(X)+b2Var(Y)+2ab Cov(X,Y).\text{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\text{Var}(X) + b^2\text{Var}(Y) + 2ab\,\text{Cov}(X,Y).Var(aX+bY)=a2Var(X)+b2Var(Y)+2abCov(X,Y).

If X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ are uncorrelated with common variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, then Var(Xˉ)=σ2/n\text{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/nVar(Xˉ)=σ2/n. With a common pairwise correlation ρ\rhoρ, Var(Xˉ)=σ2[ρ+(1−ρ)/n]\text{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2\left[\rho + (1-\rho)/n\right]Var(Xˉ)=σ2[ρ+(1−ρ)/n], so averaging correlated quantities (bagged trees, repeated measures from the same user) reduces variance less than 1/n1/n1/n and plateaus at ρσ2\rho\sigma^2ρσ2.

Other identities that come up:

  • Law of total expectation: E[X]=E[E[X∣Z]]\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z]]E[X]=E[E[X∣Z]].
  • Law of total variance: Var(X)=E[Var(X∣Z)]+Var(E[X∣Z])\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(X \mid Z)] + \text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z])Var(X)=E[Var(X∣Z)]+Var(E[X∣Z]). Used to split variance into within-group and between-group parts, for example in stratification arguments.
  • Zero correlation does not imply independence. X∼N(0,1)X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)X∼N(0,1) and Y=X2Y = X^2Y=X2 are uncorrelated yet fully dependent. The implication does hold for jointly Gaussian variables.
  • Jensen's inequality: for convex fff, E[f(X)]≥f(E[X])\mathbb{E}[f(X)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[X])E[f(X)]≥f(E[X]). Example: the sample standard deviation sss is a biased (low) estimator of σ\sigmaσ even though s2s^2s2 is unbiased for σ2\sigma^2σ2.

#Law of large numbers and central limit theorem

Let X1,X2,…X_1, X_2, \dotsX1​,X2​,… be i.i.d. with mean μ\muμ and variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, and Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ the sample mean.

Law of large numbers Central limit theorem
Statement Xˉn→μ\bar X_n \to \muXˉn​→μ as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞ n (Xˉn−μ)/σ  →d  N(0,1)\sqrt{n}\,(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1)n​(Xˉn​−μ)/σd​N(0,1)

Tells you Where the average ends up The shape and scale of fluctuations around μ\muμ: about σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​
Does not tell you How fast, or anything about the distribution of a single observation That the data are normal, or that n=30n=30n=30 is always enough

Points worth stating precisely:

  • The CLT is about the distribution of the mean (or sum), not of the data. A skewed variable stays skewed; its average becomes approximately normal.
  • How large nnn must be depends on the skewness and tails of the data. Heavy-tailed variables (revenue per user, session duration) can require far more than the folk figure of 30 for the normal approximation to be good, and with infinite variance the classical CLT does not apply at all.
  • The standard error of the mean is σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​. Halving the standard error needs four times the data.
  • Dependence breaks the standard error formula. If observations are clustered (many sessions per user) and treated as independent, standard errors are too small. Cluster at the true randomization or dependence unit.
  • The LLN is what justifies empirical risk as a proxy for true risk for a fixed hypothesis; uniform convergence over a hypothesis class needs additional machinery (see Fundamentals).
Go deeper (optional)

The Berry-Esseen theorem bounds the error of the normal approximation by a term of order ρ3/(σ3n)\rho_3/(\sigma^3\sqrt{n})ρ3​/(σ3n​), where ρ3\rho_3ρ3​ is the third absolute central moment. It quantifies why skewed or heavy-tailed data converge more slowly.

#Estimators: bias, variance, consistency

An estimator θ^\hat\thetaθ^ is a function of the sample, so it is itself a random variable with a sampling distribution. Three properties:

Property Definition Intuition
Bias Bias(θ^)=E[θ^]−θ\text{Bias}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] - \thetaBias(θ^)=E[θ^]−θ Systematic miss, averaged over repeated samples
Variance Var(θ^)\text{Var}(\hat\theta)Var(θ^) How much the estimate moves from sample to sample
Consistency θ^n→pθ\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \thetaθ^n​p​θ as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞ Converges to the truth with more data

These combine in the mean squared error:

MSE(θ^)=E[(θ^−θ)2]=Bias(θ^)2+Var(θ^).\text{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2] = \text{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2 + \text{Var}(\hat\theta).MSE(θ^)=E[(θ^−θ)2]=Bias(θ^)2+Var(θ^).

This is the same decomposition behind the bias-variance trade-off in Fundamentals, here applied to a parameter estimate instead of a prediction.

Examples:

  • The sample mean is unbiased and consistent for μ\muμ (finite variance).
  • The sample variance with divisor nnn is biased: E ⁣[1n∑(Xi−Xˉ)2]=n−1nσ2\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum (X_i-\bar X)^2\right] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2E[n1​∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2]=nn−1​σ2. Dividing by n−1n-1n−1 (Bessel's correction) removes the bias, because Xˉ\bar XXˉ is fitted from the same data, costing one degree of freedom. The biased version is still consistent.
  • Unbiased does not mean better. Ridge regression or shrinkage estimators accept bias to cut variance and can have lower MSE.
  • Consistent does not mean unbiased: a consistent estimator can be biased at every finite nnn (the divisor-nnn variance above).
  • An unbiased estimator need not be consistent (for example, using only the first observation X1X_1X1​ to estimate μ\muμ).

Under regularity conditions, the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient, attaining the Cramér-Rao lower bound Var(θ^)≥1/(n I(θ))\text{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/(n\,I(\theta))Var(θ^)≥1/(nI(θ)) with I(θ)I(\theta)I(θ) the Fisher information per observation.

#MLE vs MAP

Maximum likelihood picks the parameter that makes the observed data most probable:

θ^MLE=arg⁡max⁡θ  ∑i=1nlog⁡p(xi∣θ).\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \; \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i \mid \theta).θ^MLE​=argθmax​i=1∑n​logp(xi​∣θ).

Maximum a posteriori adds a prior p(θ)p(\theta)p(θ):

θ^MAP=arg⁡max⁡θ  [∑i=1nlog⁡p(xi∣θ)+log⁡p(θ)].\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \; \left[\sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta)\right].θ^MAP​=argθmax​[i=1∑n​logp(xi​∣θ)+logp(θ)].

The prior acts as a regularizer, and MAP reduces to MLE under a flat prior.

Model / prior Resulting objective
Gaussian noise, no prior MLE equals least squares (minimize squared error)
Gaussian noise + Gaussian prior on weights N(0,τ2I)\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2 I)N(0,τ2I) MAP equals ridge (L2), with λ=σ2/τ2\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2λ=σ2/τ2
Gaussian noise + Laplace prior on weights MAP equals lasso (L1)
Bernoulli outputs with sigmoid link, no prior MLE equals minimizing cross-entropy (logistic loss)

Worked example: for a Bernoulli parameter with kkk successes in nnn trials, p^MLE=k/n\hat p_{\text{MLE}} = k/np^​MLE​=k/n. With a Beta(α,β)\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)Beta(α,β) prior, p^MAP=k+α−1n+α+β−2\hat p_{\text{MAP}} = \dfrac{k+\alpha-1}{n+\alpha+\beta-2}p^​MAP​=n+α+β−2k+α−1​ (for α,β>1\alpha,\beta>1α,β>1). With 0 successes in 3 trials, the MLE says p=0p=0p=0, which is usually an overconfident claim, while the MAP is pulled toward the prior mean.

Interview angle

"Why is cross-entropy the right loss for classification?" is answered by MLE: minimizing it is maximizing the Bernoulli/categorical likelihood. "What is L2 regularization, probabilistically?" is MAP under a Gaussian prior. The prior's influence fades as nnn grows, so MAP and MLE agree asymptotically in standard settings. See Regression Methods for the regularization side.

Go deeper (optional)

MAP is a point summary of the posterior and is not invariant under reparameterization (the mode of a density changes with the parameterization), whereas the MLE is invariant. A fully Bayesian treatment keeps the whole posterior, for example to report credible intervals or to average predictions.

#Confidence intervals

A confidence interval is a procedure that, over repeated sampling, produces intervals containing the true parameter with the stated long-run frequency. For a mean with large nnn (or normal data with known variance):

xˉ  ±  z1−α/2  sn,z0.975≈1.96 for 95%.\bar x \;\pm\; z_{1-\alpha/2}\;\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad z_{0.975} \approx 1.96 \text{ for 95\%}.xˉ±z1−α/2​n​s​,z0.975​≈1.96 for 95%.

With normal data and unknown variance, replace zzz by the ttt quantile with n−1n-1n−1 degrees of freedom, which has heavier tails for small nnn. For a proportion, a Wald interval is p^±zp^(1−p^)/n\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}p^​±zp^​(1−p^​)/n​; it behaves poorly for small nnn or p^\hat pp^​ near 0 or 1, where Wilson or exact (Clopper-Pearson) intervals have better coverage.

Correct interpretation. The parameter is fixed and the interval is random. "If we repeated the study many times, about 95% of intervals constructed this way would contain the true value." After the interval is computed, it either contains the parameter or it does not.

Incorrect readings
  • "There is a 95% probability that the true mean lies in this interval." That is a Bayesian credible-interval statement; it requires a prior and is a different object.
  • "95% of the data lie in the interval." That describes a prediction or reference range, not a CI for the mean.
  • "If two 95% CIs overlap, the difference is not significant." Overlap is a conservative, unreliable check; build a CI for the difference directly.

CI width shrinks like 1/n1/\sqrt n1/n​, grows with σ\sigmaσ, and grows with the confidence level. A CI is more informative than a bare p-value because it shows effect size and precision together. It is also dual to testing: a two-sided level-α\alphaα test of H0:θ=θ0H_0:\theta=\theta_0H0​:θ=θ0​ rejects exactly when θ0\theta_0θ0​ is outside the (1−α)(1-\alpha)(1−α) CI, for tests and intervals built from the same statistic.

#Hypothesis testing

The setup: a null hypothesis H0H_0H0​ (typically "no effect", θ=θ0\theta=\theta_0θ=θ0​) and an alternative H1H_1H1​ (two-sided θ≠θ0\theta \ne \theta_0θ=θ0​ or one-sided). Choose a test statistic whose distribution under H0H_0H0​ is known, and a significance level α\alphaα fixed before seeing data.

p-value: the probability, computed assuming H0H_0H0​ (and the model assumptions) are true, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.

What a p-value is not
  • It is not P(H0∣data)P(H_0 \mid \text{data})P(H0​∣data). Getting that requires a prior, via Bayes' theorem.
  • It is not the probability the result occurred "by chance alone" in any direct sense.
  • It is not a measure of effect size or practical importance. With large enough nnn, trivially small effects yield tiny p-values; with small nnn, large effects can yield large ones.
  • p>αp > \alphap>α is "insufficient evidence against H0H_0H0​", not evidence that H0H_0H0​ is true. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, especially when power is low.

#Error types and power

H0H_0H0​ true H1H_1H1​ true
Reject H0H_0H0​ Type I error (false positive), probability α\alphaα Correct, probability 1−β1-\beta1−β = power
Fail to reject Correct, probability 1−α1-\alpha1−α Type II error (false negative), probability β\betaβ

Power depends on four linked quantities: the effect size δ\deltaδ, the noise σ\sigmaσ, the sample size nnn, and α\alphaα. Larger δ\deltaδ, larger nnn, smaller σ\sigmaσ, or larger α\alphaα each raise power. For a two-sample comparison of means, the required per-group sample size is derived in A-B Testing & Experimentation.

Low power has a second cost besides missed effects: among results that do reach significance, effect estimates are inflated (the "winner's curse" or Type M error), and a larger fraction of significant findings are false positives. The latter follows from Bayes: the positive predictive value of a significant result depends on the prior probability that the effect is real, which is the same base-rate logic as the screening example.

Interview angle

Asked "which test?", name the outcome type (continuous, binary, count, categorical), the design (independent vs paired), and whether the sample is large enough for the normal approximation. Then state the assumption most at risk (independence of observations is usually the one that fails).

Which to use depends on the cost of a false positive. For a confirmatory decision where one wrong call is expensive (a launch decision on a single primary metric family), FWER control fits. Related practice: designate one primary metric in advance, treat guardrail and secondary metrics as supporting evidence, and treat post hoc subgroup findings as hypotheses for follow-up. #Bootstrap

The bootstrap approximates a statistic's sampling distribution by resampling the observed data with replacement.

  1. Draw BBB samples (commonly 1,000 or more) of size nnn with replacement from the data.
  2. Compute the statistic on each.
  3. Use the spread of the BBB values to estimate the standard error, or their percentiles for an interval (the percentile bootstrap).

When it is useful: statistics without a convenient closed-form variance (median, quantiles, ratios, AUC, differences in percentiles), and checking that a CLT approximation is adequate.

Conditions and limits:

  • It assumes the sample represents the population and observations are i.i.d. For dependent data, resample at the dependence unit (users, clusters) or use block bootstrap for time series.
  • It can fail for extremes such as the sample maximum, and for very heavy-tailed data where the statistic of interest is dominated by rare values.
  • It does not fix a biased or small sample; it only describes variability in the sample you have. With small nnn, percentile intervals can undercover; bias-corrected variants (BCa) help.
  • A related tool is the permutation test: shuffle group labels to simulate the null distribution of a statistic, valid under exchangeability when H0H_0H0​ says the labels are irrelevant.

Empirical Risk Minimization

Say it in 30s: We want to minimize true risk — expected loss over the real data distribution — but we can't see that distribution. So we minimize empirical risk, the average loss on our training sample, as a stand-in. ERM is "pick the hypothesis with lowest average training loss."

Why it works (and when it doesn't)

  • Justified by the i.i.d. assumption: training and future data come from the same distribution, so empirical risk estimates true risk.
  • Pure ERM overfits, so in practice we use regularized risk minimization: empirical loss + λ·penalty.
  • When i.i.d. breaks (distribution shift, autocorrelated time series, sampling bias), empirical risk becomes an optimistic, misleading estimate.

R(f)=E(x,y)∼D[L(f(x),y)]R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathcal{L}(f(x), y)\big]R(f)=E(x,y)∼D​[L(f(x),y)]

Trap: minimizing training loss is the means, not the goal. The goal is low true risk = generalization.

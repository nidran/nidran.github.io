Eigenvectors, PCA and SVD

A worked summary that walks from eigenvectors up to PCA and SVD, reusing the same small matrices throughout so the numbers stay easy to follow.

One-line summary

PCA finds the directions of greatest variance by eigendecomposing the covariance matrix; SVD on the centered data matrix gives the same principal components in one step, more stably, because XTX=VΣ2VTX^{\mathsf T}X = V\Sigma^2 V^{\mathsf T}XTX=VΣ2VT.

#Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

A square matrix acts on vectors. For most vectors it changes their direction; for a special few it only stretches them. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue.

Av=λv,A=[2112]A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}Av=λv,A=[21​12​]

Solve det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A-\lambda I)=0det(A−λI)=0:

(2−λ)2−1=λ2−4λ+3=(λ−3)(λ−1)=0(2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda-3)(\lambda-1) = 0(2−λ)2−1=λ2−4λ+3=(λ−3)(λ−1)=0

Eigenvalues λ1=3, λ2=1\lambda_1=3,\ \lambda_2=1λ1​=3, λ2​=1, with unit eigenvectors

v1=12[11],v2=12[1−1]\mathbf{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}v1​=2​1​[11​],v2​=2​1​[1−1​]

Keep the two roles separate: the eigenvector is the direction (the axis); the eigenvalue is the spread along that direction. Check: Av1=[33]=3v1A\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix} = 3\mathbf{v}_1Av1​=[33​]=3v1​ — stretched by 3, not rotated.

#Eigendecomposition

Stack the eigenvectors as columns of QQQ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of Λ\LambdaΛ. The matrix factors as rotate → scale → rotate back:

A=QΛQ−1,Q=12[111−1],Λ=[3001]A = Q\Lambda Q^{-1}, \qquad Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}A=QΛQ−1,Q=2​1​[11​1−1​],Λ=[30​01​]

Because AAA is symmetric, QQQ is orthogonal, so Q−1=QTQ^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}Q−1=QT:

A=12[111−1][3001]12[111−1]A = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}A=2​1​[11​1−1​][30​01​]2​1​[11​1−1​]

#PCA = eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix

PCA eigendecomposes the covariance matrix of the features (a d×dd\times dd×d symmetric matrix). The eigenvectors are the principal directions (the natural axes of the data); the eigenvalues are the variance along each.

C=[2112]  ⇒  PC1=12[11] (λ1=3),PC2=12[1−1] (λ2=1)C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{PC}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\ (\lambda_1=3), \quad \text{PC}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\ (\lambda_2=1)C=[21​12​]⇒PC1​=2​1​[11​] (λ1​=3),PC2​=2​1​[1−1​] (λ2​=1)

Variance explained by PC1=33+1=75%\text{PC}_1 = \dfrac{3}{3+1} = 75\%PC1​=3+13​=75%.

Why the eigenvectors land on the data's natural axes: the covariance matrix encodes how features vary together. Its eigenvectors are the directions along which it acts as pure stretching (no rotation) — exactly the directions where variance is self-contained and uncorrelated, i.e. the directions of maximum spread.

#Why do this? (not just dimensionality reduction)

Ranking the directions of variance is the core operation. Dimensionality reduction is the common payoff, but the same computation also gives:

  • Decorrelation — the new axes are uncorrelated by construction.
  • Denoising / compression — low-variance directions are often noise and can be dropped.
  • Visualization — project many features down to the top 2–3 components to plot.
  • Interpretable structure — the principal directions themselves can be meaningful.

PCA is worth it because it finds the best directions to keep, not just any.

#SVD — decomposition of the data matrix directly

SVD factors the data matrix itself (any shape, samples × features), not the covariance matrix:

M=UΣVTM = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}M=UΣVT

  • VVV = principal directions (same axes PCA finds).
  • Σ\SigmaΣ = singular values, sorted largest first — the spread along each direction, equal to covariance eigenvalues\sqrt{\text{covariance eigenvalues}}covariance eigenvalues​ up to the n−1n-1n−1 scaling.
  • UUU = where each data point sits along the new axes.

Small example. For M=[0210]M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}M=[01​20​], the singular values come from MTMM^{\mathsf T}MMTM:

MTM=[1004]⇒eig 4,1⇒σ=2,1M^{\mathsf T}M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \Rightarrow \text{eig } 4,1 \Rightarrow \sigma = 2,1MTM=[10​04​]⇒eig 4,1⇒σ=2,1

U=[1001],Σ=[2001],VT=[0110]U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad V^{\mathsf T} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}U=[10​01​],Σ=[20​01​],VT=[01​10​]

Read right to left: VTV^{\mathsf T}VT swaps the axes, Σ\SigmaΣ scales by 2 and 1, UUU leaves them in place.

Reduction. Singular values come out ranked, so keeping the first kkk columns of each factor gives M≈UkΣkVkTM \approx U_k \Sigma_k V_k^{\mathsf T}M≈Uk​Σk​VkT​, which by the Eckart–Young theorem is the best possible rank-kkk approximation. Real algorithms never form MTMM^{\mathsf T}MMTM; they work on MMM directly, which is more numerically stable.

Why it works: XTX=VΣTUTUΣVT=VΣ2VTX^{\mathsf T}X = V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}U^{\mathsf T}U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T} = V\Sigma^2 V^{\mathsf T}XTX=VΣTUTUΣVT=VΣ2VT. Dividing by n−1n-1n−1 is exactly the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix, so SVD on centered data is PCA — in one step, and more stably than squaring XXX first.

#Key caveats

  • Centering is mandatory for PCA. SVD runs on any matrix, but uncentered data makes the first singular vector swing toward the mean (the offset from the origin) instead of the direction of maximum variance.
  • Eigenvector sign is arbitrary — v\mathbf{v}v and −v-\mathbf{v}−v are both valid; software may return either.
  • Scale features (divide each column by its std) when units differ, or the large-unit feature dominates.
  • The matrix is data, not weights — nothing is trained, there are no labels; PCA is unsupervised and the eigenvectors are the output.

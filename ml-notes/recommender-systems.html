Recommender Systems

System Design: the modeling methods behind recommendation. The architecture view lives elsewhere; this chapter is the ML-methods angle.

For the end-to-end shape (candidate generation, ranking, re-ranking, serving, feature stores) use ML System Design Framework and ML System Design Interview Prep. This chapter covers what those pages assume you can explain: how the models work, how they are trained, how they fail, and how they are evaluated.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Contrast user-based/item-based collaborative filtering with matrix factorization, and explain ALS and implicit feedback.
  • Describe a two-tower retriever, in-batch negatives, and why logQ correction is needed.
  • Choose between pointwise, pairwise and listwise ranking losses.
  • Design a multi-task ranker and combine its heads into one score.
  • Name the biases in logged data (position, selection, exposure) and the standard mitigations.
  • Pick and compute the right offline metric, and say why offline gains may not transfer online.

#The Problem Setup

A user-item interaction matrix R∈R∣U∣×∣I∣R \in \mathbb{R}^{|U|\times|I|}R∈R∣U∣×∣I∣ is extremely sparse (commonly well under 1% observed). The task is to score unobserved pairs and return a short ordered list. Two features of the data shape everything:

  • Feedback is mostly implicit: clicks, watch time, purchases, skips. Absence of an interaction is ambiguous: not seen, seen and ignored, or disliked.
  • Feedback is biased by the system that produced it: users only interact with what was shown, and in the position it was shown.
Stage Goal Typical model Scale of candidates
Retrieval (candidate generation) High recall, cheap Two-tower + approximate nearest neighbor, CF, co-occurrence Millions to billions down to hundreds or thousands
Ranking Precise ordering with rich features Deep ranker with cross-features, multi-task Hundreds to thousands down to tens
Re-ranking Business rules, diversity, fairness, calibration Slate-level heuristics or models Tens

#Collaborative Filtering

Collaborative filtering (CF) uses only the interaction pattern: users who behaved alike in the past will probably behave alike again.

Memory-based (neighborhood) methods

  • User-based: predict r^ui=rˉu+∑v∈N(u)s(u,v)(rvi−rˉv)∑v∈N(u)∣s(u,v)∣\hat r_{ui} = \bar r_u + \frac{\sum_{v\in N(u)} s(u,v)(r_{vi}-\bar r_v)}{\sum_{v\in N(u)}|s(u,v)|}r^ui​=rˉu​+∑v∈N(u)​∣s(u,v)∣∑v∈N(u)​s(u,v)(rvi​−rˉv​)​ with similarity sss (cosine, Pearson).
  • Item-based: use similarity between items computed from co-engagement. Items are typically more stable and numerous users per item give better statistics, so item-based is often preferred when users greatly outnumber items.

Strengths: simple, interpretable, strong baseline. Weaknesses: poor on sparse data, expensive at scale without approximation, no handling of cold start.

Model-based methods learn compact representations, the most important family being matrix factorization.

#Matrix Factorization

Represent each user and item by a kkk-dimensional vector (kkk typically tens to a few hundred) and predict by inner product plus biases:

#Implicit feedback

With clicks or plays there are no true negatives. The common formulation (weighted matrix factorization for implicit data) defines a preference pui=1[interaction]p_{ui}=\mathbb{1}[\text{interaction}]pui​=1[interaction] and a confidence cui=1+α ruic_{ui}=1+\alpha\, r_{ui}cui​=1+αrui​ (for example from interaction count), then minimizes over all pairs:

min⁡ ∑u,icui(pui−xu⊤yi)2+λ(∑u∥xu∥2+∑i∥yi∥2)\min\ \sum_{u,i} c_{ui}\big(p_{ui}-x_u^\top y_i\big)^2 + \lambda\big(\sum_u\|x_u\|^2+\sum_i\|y_i\|^2\big)min u,i∑​cui​(pui​−xu⊤​yi​)2+λ(u∑​∥xu​∥2+i∑​∥yi​∥2)

Summing over all pairs looks quadratic in cost, but ALS algebra reuses Y⊤YY^\top YY⊤Y so the per-user solve costs scale with the user's number of observed items rather than the whole catalog. Alternatives treat unobserved items as sampled negatives and optimize a ranking objective such as Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR), described below.

Interview angle

A common probe: "why not treat missing entries as zeros and fit squared error?" The answer: they are not negatives, they are unknown, and fitting zeros pulls scores of unseen but good items down. Implicit-feedback MF handles it with confidence weighting (observed entries high confidence, unobserved low) or with negative sampling and a ranking loss.

Go deeper

MF with dot product is a special case of a two-tower model with linear towers and one-hot IDs as inputs. Neural models generalize it by replacing each tower with a network that can take side features; this is what lets them score new items and users through content features.

#Two-Tower Retrieval

A two-tower (dual encoder) model computes a user embedding u=fθ(user, context)u = f_\theta(\text{user, context})u=fθ​(user, context) and an item embedding v=gϕ(item)v = g_\phi(\text{item})v=gϕ​(item), scores by a dot product or cosine, and trains so that matched pairs score higher than unmatched ones. Because item embeddings do not depend on the user, they are computed offline and indexed in an approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) structure; serving is one user-tower forward pass plus an ANN lookup.

The price of this factorization is expressiveness: the interaction between user and item features is restricted to a single inner product, so fine-grained cross-features are left to the ranker.

#Training with sampled softmax

Treat retrieval as extreme classification over the catalog. With a positive item iii and scores s(u,j)=u⊤vjs(u,j)=u^\top v_js(u,j)=u⊤vj​, the full softmax loss is

L=−log⁡exp⁡(s(u,i)/τ)∑j∈Iexp⁡(s(u,j)/τ)\mathcal{L} = -\log\frac{\exp(s(u,i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j\in I}\exp(s(u,j)/\tau)}L=−log∑j∈I​exp(s(u,j)/τ)exp(s(u,i)/τ)​

which is too expensive for large catalogs, so the denominator is approximated using a sampled set of negatives. A temperature τ\tauτ controls how sharply hard negatives are emphasized.

#Negative sampling

Scheme How Strength Risk
Random negatives Uniform over catalog Cheap, unbiased view of the catalog Mostly easy negatives; weak learning signal
In-batch negatives Other positives in the same batch serve as negatives for each example Free (reuses computed embeddings), scales batch-wise Sampling is proportional to item popularity, biasing the model against popular items; false negatives when the same item appears twice
Hard negatives Items ranked highly by the current model but not interacted with; or ANN-mined Sharper decision boundary Can include unlabeled true positives (false negatives); too-hard negatives destabilize training
Impression negatives Shown but not clicked Matches the serving distribution Biased by the previous ranker; position bias
Mixed Combination (for example in-batch plus random plus a small share of hard) Balanced More hyperparameters

#Why logQ correction

With in-batch negatives, an item jjj appears as a negative with probability proportional to its frequency qjq_jqj​ in the training data (items that are popular are frequent in batches). The model then sees popular items as negatives far more often than they would appear under uniform sampling, so it learns to push them down, and the retrieval ends up under-recommending popular items relative to the true objective.

The standard fix subtracts the log of the sampling probability from each logit, estimating the full-softmax objective from a non-uniform sample:

sc(u,j)=s(u,j)−log⁡qjs^{c}(u,j) = s(u,j) - \log q_jsc(u,j)=s(u,j)−logqj​

so the training denominator becomes ∑jexp⁡(s(u,j)−log⁡qj)\sum_j \exp(s(u,j)-\log q_j)∑j​exp(s(u,j)−logqj​). Intuitively, a negative that was oversampled by factor qjq_jqj​ is downweighted by 1/qj1/q_j1/qj​ (importance correction). qjq_jqj​ is estimated from streaming item counts. At serving time the uncorrected score s(u,j)s(u,j)s(u,j) is used. The correction reduces a systematic bias but can increase variance for rare items, and the estimate of qjq_jqj​ must track drift.

Common pitfalls

Mixing up train and serve distributions (training only on clicked impressions while serving over the whole catalog), forgetting L2-normalization when using cosine with a temperature, and evaluating retrieval with a model whose ANN index is stale relative to the item tower.

#Ranking Models

The ranker takes a few hundred candidates and uses rich user, item, context and cross features, typically a deep network (feature crosses via factorization machines, cross networks, or attention over the user history) with an output layer per objective.

#Learning-to-rank losses

Family Training signal Example losses Pros Cons
Pointwise Each (user, item) independently Log loss for click, regression for watch time Simple, calibrated probabilities, easy to combine with business logic Ignores relative order; optimizes a proxy of ranking quality
Pairwise Pairs where one item should beat another BPR: −log⁡σ(si−sj)-\log\sigma(s_i - s_j)−logσ(si​−sj​); hinge; RankNet Directly targets ordering Pair construction and bias; O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) pairs; calibration lost
Listwise A whole ranked list ListNet (softmax cross-entropy over the list), LambdaRank / LambdaMART (gradients weighted by the metric change from swapping two items) Can align with metrics like NDCG More complex; needs list-level data; calibration lost

Notes on choosing:

  • Pointwise logistic loss gives probabilities usable downstream (for example in expected-value formulas or ads auctions). Pairwise and listwise losses give only scores good for ordering; calibrate separately if probabilities are needed.
  • LambdaRank-style weights multiply the pairwise gradient by ∣ΔNDCG∣|\Delta \text{NDCG}|∣ΔNDCG∣ of swapping the two items, which concentrates effort on the top of the list. This is a heuristic gradient rather than the gradient of a smooth loss, though it is observed to improve NDCG in many settings.
  • All three can be hybridized: for example a pointwise head plus a pairwise auxiliary loss.

#Multi-task and multi-objective ranking

A single click probability rewards clickbait. Production rankers typically predict several outcomes: click, long watch, like, share, comment, hide/dislike, purchase.

Architecture options

Design Idea Trade-off
Shared-bottom One shared trunk, one head per task Efficient, but conflicting tasks can hurt each other (negative transfer)
Mixture-of-experts (MMoE-style) Several expert networks; a per-task gate mixes experts More flexible for tasks with different relatedness; costlier, gates can collapse
Separate models Independent model per task No interference; higher cost, no sharing for sparse tasks

Combining heads into one ranking score is usually a product-defined formula, for example a weighted sum of predicted probabilities, score=∑kwk p^k\text{score} = \sum_k w_k\,\hat p_kscore=∑k​wk​p^​k​, or a multiplicative form such as p^clicka p^likeb\hat p_{\text{click}}^{a}\,\hat p_{\text{like}}^{b}p^​clicka​p^​likeb​ minus penalties for negative signals. Weights encode business priorities and are tuned with online experiments, because an offline metric cannot tell which weighting improves long-term satisfaction. Negative feedback (hide, report) is often given a large negative weight because it is rare but costly.

Training considerations: tasks with very different label frequencies need loss weighting or separate sampling; delayed labels (purchases) cause label-lag bias; and calibration per head matters when the combination formula assumes comparable probabilities.

Interview angle

When asked "what do you optimize?", avoid a single click metric. A strong answer states the ranking score is a function of several predicted outcomes, justifies each (engagement, satisfaction, negative feedback), and explains the weights are product decisions validated by A/B tests with guardrail metrics.

#Cold Start

Case Approach
New item Content-based features (text, image, category, creator) fed to the item tower; exploration quota or boost for new items; use creator-level priors; fast embedding updates as interactions arrive
New user Onboarding questions or imported signals; start from popular or demographic/contextual priors (location, device, time); exploit the first few interactions with a session-based model
New system Heuristics and popularity, then simple CF as data accumulates

ID-based embeddings have no signal for unseen IDs, so hybrid models combine ID embeddings with content features and fall back to content features when the ID is new (for example with ID dropout during training so the model learns to rely on content when the ID is uninformative).

#Exploration

A system that only shows its top predictions never learns about items it currently underrates; the logged data then reinforces its own beliefs. Exploration deliberately gathers information at some short-term cost. Practical forms:

  • Randomization within a small traffic slice, or random insertion into low-stakes slots.
  • Bandit-style selection for new or uncertain items: see Bandits & Reinforcement Learning Basics for epsilon-greedy, UCB and Thompson sampling.
  • Uncertainty-aware scoring (for example ensembles or dropout-based variance used as an optimism bonus).
  • Logging the propensity (probability with which each item was shown) so the data can later be used for unbiased off-policy learning and evaluation.

#Biases in Logged Data

Bias What happens Mitigations
Position bias Higher slots get more clicks regardless of relevance Feature the position in training and set it to a default at serving; inverse propensity weighting by examination probability; a separate position tower trained jointly and dropped at inference; randomized-position experiments to estimate propensities
Selection / exposure bias Only shown items receive labels; the unlabeled items are not negatives Train on impressions with propensity correction; exploration traffic; treat missing as unknown
Popularity bias Popular items dominate positives, then dominate recommendations logQ correction, popularity-aware negative sampling, re-ranking for exposure
Conformity / presentation bias Thumbnails, titles and context change clicks Include presentation features, use deeper engagement signals
Survivorship / feedback bias Only active users generate data Weight by user inclusion probability, track cohorts that left

Inverse propensity scoring (IPS) reweights each logged example by 1/πi1/\pi_i1/πi​ where πi\pi_iπi​ is the probability that the item was exposed or examined. The estimator is unbiased when propensities are correct and positive wherever relevance is non-zero, but variance explodes for small propensities, so weights are typically clipped.

Go deeper

The joint position-bias tower works because during training the model can attribute some of the click to position and the rest to relevance; dropping it at serving yields a score meant to reflect relevance only. It relies on position being sufficiently randomized or otherwise identifiable from the features; if the ranker's own scores determine position, the two effects are confounded and the decomposition is not identified.

Interpreting them: MRR focuses on the first hit only; MAP rewards placing all relevant items early; NDCG handles graded labels and the logarithmic discount; recall@K is rank-insensitive within the top KKK. See Precision & Recall for the underlying tradeoff.

Evaluation pitfalls

Random splits leak the future: use time-based splits (train on the past, test on later interactions), since random splitting lets the model see a user's later behavior when predicting earlier behavior. Evaluating against logged clicks measures agreement with the old policy, not true relevance. Sampled metrics (ranking the positive against a few hundred random negatives instead of the whole catalog) can order models differently from full-catalog metrics. Also check per-segment performance (new users, tail items) because an average can hide regressions.

#Online evaluation

Offline metrics are proxies. The decision metric comes from A/B tests (or interleaving for faster sensitivity in ranking comparisons) on engagement and retention, with guardrails such as latency, negative feedback rate, creator-side health and diversity. Offline-online gaps arise from feedback loops, position bias in logged labels, novelty effects, and objectives that the offline metric does not capture. Long-term effects (retention, satisfaction) often need holdouts running for weeks.

#Diversity, Fairness and Feedback Loops

Diversity and novelty. Ranking by predicted relevance alone tends to produce near-duplicate lists. Re-ranking methods trade relevance against redundancy: Maximal Marginal Relevance picks the next item maximizing λ rel(i)−(1−λ)max⁡j∈Ssim(i,j)\lambda\,\text{rel}(i) - (1-\lambda)\max_{j\in S}\text{sim}(i,j)λrel(i)−(1−λ)maxj∈S​sim(i,j); determinantal point processes model set-level diversity; simple rules cap items per creator or category. Diversity is measured by intra-list distance, coverage, or category entropy, and it is justified by user value (variety, satisfaction) rather than assumed free.

Fairness. Several stakeholders exist: users (quality across groups), item providers (exposure), and the platform. Provider-side fairness often targets exposure proportional to merit under some definition, which is a policy choice with trade-offs, not a single formula. Common tools: auditing metrics per group, constrained re-ranking for exposure, reducing popularity bias, and avoiding proxies for protected attributes in features. The correct definition depends on the product and legal context.

Feedback loops. The model's recommendations determine what users see, which determines the next training data, which updates the model. Effects include popularity concentration, filter bubbles, and drift toward what is easy to engage with. Mitigations: logged propensities and exploration, counterfactual or off-policy corrections, holdout traffic served by a randomized or older policy for unbiased evaluation, diversity constraints, and monitoring distribution shift in exposure and in item and user cohorts.

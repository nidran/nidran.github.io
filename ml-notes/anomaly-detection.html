Anomaly Detection

Finding the rare, the unusual, and the broken, usually with few or no labels.

An anomaly is an observation that deviates from what the data-generating process normally produces. Practical uses include fraud, equipment faults, intrusion, data-quality problems, and rare disease signals. Three features make the problem hard and interview-worthy: anomalies are rare (extreme class imbalance), they are heterogeneous (there is no single "anomalous" distribution to learn), and labels are scarce and biased. Most of the craft is in framing the problem and in evaluating honestly, not in the choice of algorithm.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Distinguish outlier detection from novelty detection, and supervised from unsupervised framings.
  • Explain the statistical, distance, density (LOF), isolation, one-class SVM, and reconstruction approaches, with the condition under which each fails.
  • Derive the Isolation Forest score and say why short paths mean anomalous.
  • Evaluate a detector with scarce labels using PR-AUC and precision@k, and set a threshold from an alert budget.
  • Describe how drift and feedback loops degrade detectors in production.

#Outlier detection versus novelty detection

Outlier detection Novelty detection
Training data Contains a (usually small) unknown fraction of anomalies Assumed clean: only normal examples
Goal Flag the abnormal points in the dataset itself Decide whether a new point is unlike the training data
Typical fit Robust methods (isolation forest, LOF, robust covariance) One-class models trained on normal-only data
Risk Anomalies distort the model of normal "Clean" data is rarely clean; contamination biases the boundary

#Supervised, semi-supervised, unsupervised

Framing Labels needed Works when Weakness
Supervised (a classifier on labeled anomaly vs normal) Many labeled anomalies Anomaly types are known, stable, and well represented Misses novel types; labels lag behind new attacks or faults
Semi-supervised (model normal, perhaps tune with a few labels) Normal examples; a few anomalies for tuning Normal behavior is well sampled Needs clean-ish data
Unsupervised (score everything; no labels) None Anomalies are rare and differ from the bulk Hard to evaluate; the notion of "unusual" is a modeling choice

A pragmatic rule: with enough labeled examples of the failure types you care about, a supervised classifier (gradient boosting with class weights or resampling; see Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting) usually outperforms unsupervised scores on those known types. Unsupervised scores are most valuable for unknown unknowns, as a feature into the supervised model, and for bootstrapping labels. Hybrid systems commonly use both: a supervised model for known patterns and an unsupervised detector as a safety net, whose top alerts feed review and then become new labels.

Interview angle

Start by asking: what is an anomaly here, what does a miss cost versus a false alarm, how many alerts can people review per day, and are there any labels (and how were they obtained)? These answers choose the framing, the metric, and the threshold before any algorithm is mentioned.

Parametric univariate. Under approximate normality, flag ∣z∣=∣x−xˉ∣/s|z|=|x-\bar x|/s∣z∣=∣x−xˉ∣/s above a cutoff (3 is a common convention, not a law). Density models. Fit a density p^(x)\hat p(x)p^​(x) (a GMM, kernel density estimate, or normalizing flow) and flag low p^(x)\hat p(x)p^​(x). Time series. For temporal data, remove trend and seasonality first (otherwise every Monday morning is "anomalous"), then score residuals; see Time Series Stationarity. Contextual anomalies, where a value is normal in one context and abnormal in another (a high load at 3 a.m.), need context in the model.

#Distance-based methods

k-nearest-neighbor score. Score each point by its distance to its kkk-th nearest neighbor (or the mean distance to its kkk nearest neighbors). Isolated points have large scores. It is simple and nonparametric, but costs O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) naively (indexes help in low dimensions), needs scaled features, degrades in high dimensions as distances concentrate, and uses one global notion of "far", which is a problem when normal regions differ in density. That last limitation motivates LOF.

#Density-based: Local Outlier Factor (LOF)

LOF compares a point's local density with the densities of its neighbors, so it can flag a point that is unusual relative to its own neighborhood even when it would be unremarkable in a sparser region elsewhere.

For a point aaa, let k-dist(b)k\text{-dist}(b)k-dist(b) be the distance from bbb to its kkk-th neighbor and Nk(a)N_k(a)Nk​(a) its kkk-neighborhood.

Like other distance methods it needs scaling and suffers in high dimensions, and the score has no absolute scale across datasets. In the novelty-detection setting, fit on normal data and score new points.

#Isolation Forest

Idea. Anomalies are "few and different", so they are easier to isolate with random cuts. Build many random trees: at each node, pick a feature at random and a split value uniformly between that feature's min and max within the node's data; recurse until a point is alone (or a depth limit is reached). Anomalies end up isolated after fewer splits; normal points in dense regions need many.

Score. Let h(x)h(x)h(x) be the path length (number of edges) to isolate xxx in one tree and E[h(x)]E[h(x)]E[h(x)] its average over the forest. Normalize by the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree on ψ\psiψ points:

c(ψ)=2H(ψ−1)−2(ψ−1)ψ,H(i)≈ln⁡i+0.5772 (Euler’s constant),c(\psi)=2H(\psi-1)-\frac{2(\psi-1)}{\psi},\qquad H(i)\approx\ln i+0.5772\ (\text{Euler's constant}),c(ψ)=2H(ψ−1)−ψ2(ψ−1)​,H(i)≈lni+0.5772 (Euler’s constant),

s(x,ψ)=2−E[h(x)]/c(ψ).s(x,\psi)=2^{-E[h(x)]/c(\psi)} .s(x,ψ)=2−E[h(x)]/c(ψ).

Reading sss: E[h]→0E[h]\to0E[h]→0 gives s→1s\to1s→1 (anomalous); E[h]=c(ψ)E[h]=c(\psi)E[h]=c(ψ) gives s=0.5s=0.5s=0.5 (typical); E[h]≫c(ψ)E[h]\gg c(\psi)E[h]≫c(ψ) gives s→0s\to0s→0 (clearly normal). If all points score close to 0.50.50.5, the data contain no distinct anomalies.

Why it scales. Each tree is built on a small subsample (the original paper uses ψ=256\psi=256ψ=256 and about 100 trees), with trees capped at depth about ⌈log⁡2ψ⌉\lceil\log_2\psi\rceil⌈log2​ψ⌉. Subsampling reduces swamping (normal points near anomalies being mislabeled) and masking (clusters of anomalies hiding one another) and makes training roughly linear in nnn with low memory. No distance computation is needed.

Limits.

  • Splits are axis-parallel, so anomalies that are unusual only through a combination of correlated features along a diagonal may get artifacts in score maps (the Extended Isolation Forest uses random hyperplanes to address this).
  • Irrelevant features dilute the random splits; remove noise features or use feature selection.
  • Local anomalies hidden inside dense regions of another cluster can be missed, since isolation is global rather than relative to local density (LOF handles this better).
  • The contamination parameter only sets the threshold (a quantile of scores), not the model; if it is wrong, the cutoff is wrong.
  • Categorical features need encoding, and the encoding affects splits.

#One-class SVM

A one-class SVM learns a boundary around normal data in a kernel feature space. In the ν\nuν-formulation (Schölkopf et al.) it separates the data from the origin with maximum margin:

and the decision function is f(x)=sign(w⋅ϕ(x)−ρ)f(x)=\text{sign}(w\cdot\phi(x)-\rho)f(x)=sign(w⋅ϕ(x)−ρ). The parameter ν∈(0,1]\nu\in(0,1]ν∈(0,1] is an upper bound on the fraction of training points treated as outliers and a lower bound on the fraction of support vectors, which makes it interpretable. With an RBF kernel the boundary can wrap flexible shapes. Conditions and cautions: training is roughly between quadratic and cubic in nnn, which limits it to moderate datasets; results are sensitive to the kernel width γ\gammaγ and to feature scaling; it assumes mostly normal training data (it is a novelty-detection tool) and degrades when contaminated; and it offers little guidance for high-dimensional or heterogeneous data. See Support Vector Machines for the margin and kernel background.

#Reconstruction-based: autoencoders and PCA

Train a model to reconstruct normal data through a bottleneck; at test time, large reconstruction error ∥x−x^∥\lVert x-\hat x\rVert∥x−x^∥ suggests that xxx does not follow the learned structure. PCA reconstruction error (distance to the principal subspace) is the linear baseline and often competitive; see Eigenvectors, PCA and SVD. An autoencoder generalizes this to nonlinear manifolds (see Clustering & Dimensionality Reduction).

Conditions and cautions:

  • The premise is that the model reconstructs normal data well and anomalies poorly. An over-capacity autoencoder can learn an approximate identity and reconstruct anomalies too, especially if the training data are contaminated. Control capacity, use denoising or regularization, and monitor reconstruction on a validation set.
  • Error is dominated by feature scale and by inherently noisy features; scale inputs and consider per-feature normalization of the error.
  • Reconstruction error can be small for anomalies that lie close to the learned manifold (an anomaly that is "simple") and large for normal but hard examples; it measures complexity as well as abnormality.
  • Variants: VAEs (use the ELBO or reconstruction probability), sequence autoencoders for time series, and forecasting-residual models.

An effective baseline order for tabular data: robust statistical rules for sanity, isolation forest as the first multivariate detector, LOF or a robust Mahalanobis check as a complement, and a supervised model once labels accrue.

#Why accuracy and ROC-AUC mislead

If 0.1%0.1\%0.1% of points are anomalous, predicting "normal" gets 99.9%99.9\%99.9% accuracy. ROC-AUC uses the false positive rate, whose denominator is the huge number of negatives, so a detector can show a high ROC-AUC while most of its alerts are false (see ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC). Precision is what the reviewing human experiences.

Metric Use Note
PR-AUC (average precision) Threshold-free ranking quality with rare positives Baseline equals the prevalence, so compare against it, not against 0.50.50.5
Precision@k, recall@k Quality of the top kkk alerts, where kkk is the review budget Directly maps to operations; depends on kkk
Recall at fixed precision (or precision at fixed recall) Business operating point Needs enough labels to estimate stably

#Label problems

  • Selection bias. If only flagged cases are investigated, the labeled set reflects what the current system flags; anomalies the system misses are labeled "normal" or never labeled. Estimates of recall are therefore optimistic. Mitigations: audit a random sample of unflagged cases, and keep a small randomized exploration channel.
  • Label noise and delay. Fraud labels can arrive weeks late; "normal" is often just "not yet reported". Evaluate on data old enough to be labeled, and expect recent performance to be underestimated.
  • Synthetic anomalies. Injecting artificial outliers is useful for smoke tests but measures how well the detector finds the type you injected; the number is not a forecast of real performance.
  • Few positives mean wide uncertainty. With tens of positives, PR-AUC and precision@k have large variance; report bootstrap intervals and avoid over-tuning on them.
  • Time-aware splits. Validate with forward-chaining splits, since anomalies and normal behavior shift over time (see Cross-Validation).

When no labels exist, use proxies: manual review of the top-kkk with a rubric, stability of the top set across seeds and subsamples, agreement among detectors, and sensitivity to injected known anomalies.

#Thresholding

An anomaly score is a ranking; an action needs a threshold. Options:

Approach How Condition and caveat
Alert budget Flag the top qqq percent or top kkk per day Fits human review capacity; ignores whether the day is truly more anomalous
Contamination quantile Choose the cutoff at the expected anomaly fraction Depends on a prevalence estimate that is usually uncertain
Statistical χ2\chi^2χ2 quantile for Mahalanobis; ±3\pm3±3 MAD-based Valid only if distributional assumptions hold
Extreme value theory Fit a generalized Pareto distribution to the score tail (peaks-over-threshold) to set a tail-probability cutoff Needs enough tail data; assumes tail stationarity
Cost-based Pick the threshold minimizing expected cost on validation data Needs labels and cost estimates
Segmented thresholds Separate thresholds per segment (device type, region, hour) Prevents a heterogeneous population from being dominated by its noisiest segment; needs enough data per segment

Consider two-stage designs: a cheap detector with a loose threshold produces candidates, and a stronger model or human review makes the final call. Always report how threshold choice changes alert volume, because that is what determines whether the system is usable.

#Drift, concept change, and feedback loops

  • Normal changes. Seasonality, product launches, and infrastructure changes shift the baseline; a detector trained on old data flags the new normal en masse (a false-alarm flood) or, if it adapts silently, absorbs a genuine fault into "normal". Use rolling or recent training windows, seasonality-aware features, and scheduled retraining; compare adaptation speed with the speed of the faults you must catch.
  • Monitor the detector itself. Track the score distribution, alert rate, and input feature distributions; a sudden shift in alert rate is a drift signal as often as an incident signal. Pair the detector with data-quality checks so that a broken upstream feed is not mistaken for an anomaly (or vice versa).
  • Adversarial adaptation. In fraud and security, actors react to detection; the anomaly distribution is non-stationary by design, and a fixed model decays. Rotate features, keep unsupervised coverage, and refresh labels quickly.
  • Feedback loops. Acting on alerts changes the data (blocked transactions never get completed, so they never get confirmed labels). Keep a holdout of un-actioned cases where safe, and treat the labeled set as biased.
  • Concept versus covariate shift. If p(x)p(x)p(x) shifts but the notion of anomalous stays the same, recalibrate thresholds; if the notion itself changes, labels and the objective need revisiting. Distinguish them before retraining blindly.
Interview angle

The strongest answers on this topic spend as much time on evaluation, thresholds, and monitoring as on algorithms. A good summary: pick the metric from the review budget (precision@k and PR-AUC against the prevalence baseline), audit a random slice of unflagged cases to estimate recall, and monitor alert rate and score drift after launch.

Common pitfalls

Tuning hyperparameters (like the contamination fraction) on the same few labeled anomalies used to report performance; reporting ROC-AUC alone on a heavily imbalanced set; training a novelty detector on data that was never checked for contamination; ignoring seasonality so that scheduled behavior triggers alarms.

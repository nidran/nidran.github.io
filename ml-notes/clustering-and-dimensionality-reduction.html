Clustering & Dimensionality Reduction

Unsupervised learning: finding structure when there is no label to check against.

Unsupervised methods answer questions of the form "what is the shape of this data?" rather than "what is yyy?". Two families dominate interviews: clustering (group similar points) and dimensionality reduction / embedding (represent points with fewer coordinates). Both share one uncomfortable property: there is no ground truth, so every method embeds an assumption about what "structure" means, and a good answer names that assumption and the condition under which it fails.

What you'll be able to say after this chapter
  • Write the k-means objective, explain why Lloyd's algorithm converges, and say why k-means++ helps.
  • Derive the E and M steps of a Gaussian mixture model and state its exact relation to k-means.
  • Pick between k-means, GMM, hierarchical, DBSCAN/HDBSCAN from the shape and density of the data.
  • Evaluate a clustering without labels, and say what silhouette cannot tell you.
  • Explain what t-SNE and UMAP preserve, and what you must not read off their plots.

#Setting the scene: what "structure" can mean

Notion of structure Method family Assumption it bakes in
Compact, roughly spherical blobs k-means Euclidean distance to a centroid defines membership
Overlapping ellipsoidal blobs Gaussian mixture Data are generated from a mixture of Gaussians
Nested groups at several scales Hierarchical A tree of merges is meaningful
Dense regions separated by sparse ones DBSCAN / HDBSCAN Clusters are connected high-density regions
Low-dimensional manifold PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, autoencoder Data lie near a lower-dimensional surface

Two preprocessing facts apply to nearly everything below. First, almost all of these methods use a distance, so feature scaling changes the answer; standardize (or choose a deliberate weighting) before clustering. Second, in high dimensions pairwise distances concentrate (the ratio of nearest to farthest distance tends toward 1 for many distributions), so distance-based clustering usually works better after reducing dimension, for example with PCA (see Eigenvectors, PCA and SVD).

#Objective

Given nnn points xi∈Rdx_i \in \mathbb{R}^dxi​∈Rd and a chosen kkk, k-means seeks centroids μ1,…,μk\mu_1,\dots,\mu_kμ1​,…,μk​ and assignments c(i)∈{1,…,k}c(i)\in\{1,\dots,k\}c(i)∈{1,…,k} minimizing the within-cluster sum of squares (also called inertia or distortion):

J(c,μ)=∑i=1n∥xi−μc(i)∥2.J(c,\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lVert x_i - \mu_{c(i)} \rVert^2 .J(c,μ)=i=1∑n​∥xi​−μc(i)​∥2.

Finding the global minimum is NP-hard in general, so in practice a local-search heuristic is used.

#Lloyd's algorithm

Alternate two steps, each of which cannot increase JJJ:

  1. Assignment. With centroids fixed, assign each point to its nearest centroid: c(i)=arg⁡min⁡k∥xi−μk∥2c(i)=\arg\min_k \lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2c(i)=argmink​∥xi​−μk​∥2. This minimizes JJJ over ccc for fixed μ\muμ.
  2. Update. With assignments fixed, set each centroid to the mean of its points: μk=1∣Ck∣∑i∈Ckxi\mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i\in C_k} x_iμk​=∣Ck​∣1​∑i∈Ck​​xi​. The mean is the minimizer of the sum of squared distances to a set of points, so this minimizes JJJ over μ\muμ for fixed ccc.

This is coordinate descent on JJJ. Because JJJ is bounded below by zero and never increases, and there are finitely many partitions, the algorithm terminates at a local minimum (or fixed point). Each iteration costs O(nkd)O(nkd)O(nkd). Different initializations can give different local minima, so the standard practice is several restarts and keeping the lowest JJJ.

#Initialization: k-means++

Random initialization can place several centroids in one dense blob and leave other blobs uncovered. k-means++ seeds sequentially: choose the first centroid uniformly at random; then choose each subsequent centroid from the data with probability proportional to D(x)2D(x)^2D(x)2, the squared distance to the nearest centroid already chosen. Far-away points are more likely to be picked, which spreads the seeds. In expectation the seeding alone is O(log⁡k)O(\log k)O(logk)-competitive with the optimal objective, and in practice it usually needs fewer Lloyd iterations and gives lower JJJ than uniform seeding.

#Choosing kkk

JJJ decreases monotonically in kkk (more centroids can only fit better, reaching 000 at k=nk=nk=n), so JJJ alone cannot choose kkk.

Interview angle

When asked "how do you choose kkk?", do not stop at the elbow. Say: the elbow and silhouette are heuristics that can disagree; there may be no true kkk; so combine an internal criterion, a stability check, and whether the segments are actionable. Acknowledging that "the right kkk" can be a modeling decision rather than a fact about the data is a mark of seniority.

#Limits and failure modes

  • Voronoi geometry. Assignment to the nearest centroid means cluster boundaries are straight (hyperplanes). Non-convex shapes (rings, crescents) are split incorrectly.
  • Equal-variance, equal-size bias. Because only the mean matters, k-means tends to cut large or wide clusters to balance variance, and cannot represent clusters with different spreads or correlated features. Unequal cluster sizes can lead to a big cluster being split.
  • Scale and outliers. Squared error amplifies outliers; a few extreme points can claim a centroid. kkk-medians or kkk-medoids (PAM) are more robust, at higher cost.
  • Hard assignment. A point on a boundary gets the same label as a point at a centroid; no uncertainty is reported.
  • Euclidean only. The mean minimizing squared error is tied to Euclidean distance; other distances need other algorithms (k-medoids, spectral clustering, or cosine/spherical k-means with normalized vectors).
  • Scalability levers. Mini-batch k-means updates centroids with small random batches, trading a little objective for large speedups on big data.
A common mistake

Running k-means on raw features of different units (income in dollars, age in years). The largest-scale feature dominates the distance, so clusters reflect that feature alone. Standardize first, and for categorical data use k-modes or a mixed-type distance rather than one-hot features treated as continuous.

#The model

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) assumes each point is generated by first drawing a latent component z∼Categorical(π)z\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi)z∼Categorical(π) and then x∣z=k∼N(μk,Σk)x\mid z=k\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_k,\Sigma_k)x∣z=k∼N(μk​,Σk​). The density is

p(x)=∑k=1Kπk N(x∣μk,Σk),πk≥0, ∑kπk=1.p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k),\qquad \pi_k\ge0,\ \sum_k\pi_k=1 .p(x)=k=1∑K​πk​N(x∣μk​,Σk​),πk​≥0, k∑​πk​=1.

It is a generative model (see Generative vs Discriminative): it defines p(x)p(x)p(x), can sample, and can score new points. Maximum likelihood is

ℓ(θ)=∑i=1nlog⁡∑k=1Kπk N(xi∣μk,Σk).\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k).ℓ(θ)=i=1∑n​logk=1∑K​πk​N(xi​∣μk​,Σk​).

The logarithm of a sum does not split into per-component terms, so there is no closed-form maximizer. If the ziz_izi​ were observed, the log-likelihood would be ∑ilog⁡πziN(xi∣μzi,Σzi)\sum_i \log \pi_{z_i}\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_{z_i},\Sigma_{z_i})∑i​logπzi​​N(xi​∣μzi​​,Σzi​​), which is trivially maximized by per-group sample statistics. EM exploits this by guessing the missing ziz_izi​ softly.

#EM: the two steps

E-step. With current parameters θ(t)\theta^{(t)}θ(t), compute the responsibility of component kkk for point iii, which is the posterior probability of the latent assignment (Bayes' rule):

γik=p(zi=k∣xi,θ(t))=πk N(xi∣μk,Σk)∑jπj N(xi∣μj,Σj).\gamma_{ik}=p(z_i=k\mid x_i,\theta^{(t)})=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)} .γik​=p(zi​=k∣xi​,θ(t))=∑j​πj​N(xi​∣μj​,Σj​)πk​N(xi​∣μk​,Σk​)​.

M-step. Maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood with responsibilities as soft counts. With Nk=∑iγikN_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}Nk​=∑i​γik​:

πk=Nkn,μk=1Nk∑iγik xi,Σk=1Nk∑iγik(xi−μk)(xi−μk)T.\pi_k=\frac{N_k}{n},\qquad \mu_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\,x_i,\qquad \Sigma_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)(x_i-\mu_k)^{\mathsf T}.πk​=nNk​​,μk​=Nk​1​i∑​γik​xi​,Σk​=Nk​1​i∑​γik​(xi​−μk​)(xi​−μk​)T.

These are weighted versions of the usual sample proportion, mean, and covariance.

#Why EM works (conceptually)

For any distribution qi(k)q_i(k)qi​(k) over the latent variable, Jensen's inequality gives a lower bound (the evidence lower bound, ELBO):

log⁡p(X∣θ) ≥ ∑i∑kqi(k)log⁡p(xi,zi=k∣θ)qi(k).\log p(X\mid\theta)\ \ge\ \sum_i\sum_k q_i(k)\log\frac{p(x_i,z_i=k\mid\theta)}{q_i(k)} .logp(X∣θ) ≥ i∑​k∑​qi​(k)logqi​(k)p(xi​,zi​=k∣θ)​.

The bound is tight exactly when qi(k)q_i(k)qi​(k) equals the posterior p(zi=k∣xi,θ)p(z_i=k\mid x_i,\theta)p(zi​=k∣xi​,θ). So the E-step sets qqq to the posterior, making the bound touch the log-likelihood at the current θ\thetaθ; the M-step then raises the bound over θ\thetaθ. Since the bound sits below the likelihood and touches it at the old θ\thetaθ, the likelihood cannot decrease. EM therefore monotonically increases the likelihood and converges to a stationary point, generally a local maximum, so initialization and restarts matter (k-means is commonly used to initialize).

#Relation to k-means

k-means is a limiting special case of GMM-EM: take equal mixing weights, shared spherical covariance Σk=σ2I\Sigma_k=\sigma^2 IΣk​=σ2I, and let σ2→0\sigma^2\to0σ2→0. The responsibilities then collapse to hard 0/1 assignments to the nearest centroid (the E-step becomes the assignment step), and the M-step reduces to the mean update. Conversely, a GMM adds three things: soft assignments, per-cluster covariance and weight, and a proper probabilistic model.

Practical points.

  • Singularities. If one component centers on a single point and its covariance shrinks to zero, the density there diverges and the likelihood is unbounded. Guard with a covariance floor (add ϵI\epsilon IϵI, "reg_covar") or a prior.
  • Covariance structure. Full, diagonal, tied, and spherical covariances trade flexibility against the parameter count; with limited data and high ddd, diagonal or tied is often more stable than full.
  • Choosing KKK. Because GMM has a likelihood, BIC =−2ℓ+pln⁡n=-2\ell+p\ln n=−2ℓ+plnn (ppp free parameters) penalizes complexity; minimum BIC across a grid of KKK and covariance types is a common recipe. This presumes the Gaussian assumption is roughly right; with non-Gaussian clusters BIC may add components just to approximate a skewed shape.
  • Mixtures are also density estimators. A GMM can be used directly for anomaly scoring through low p(x)p(x)p(x) (see Anomaly Detection).
Go deeper (optional)

EM is a general recipe for latent-variable models with tractable posteriors (HMMs, factor analysis, mixtures of experts). When the posterior is intractable, one optimizes the ELBO with an approximate qqq, which is the road from EM to variational inference and VAEs.

#Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative clustering starts with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the closest pair of clusters until one remains, recording the merges in a dendrogram. Cutting the dendrogram at a height gives a flat clustering; no kkk needs to be fixed in advance, and one tree supports many resolutions. (Divisive clustering goes top-down and is less common.)

What "closest pair" means is the linkage:

Linkage Distance between clusters A,BA,BA,B Behavior
Single min⁡a∈A,b∈Bd(a,b)\min_{a\in A,b\in B}d(a,b)mina∈A,b∈B​d(a,b) Follows thin chains; "chaining" merges distinct clusters joined by a bridge of points; good for elongated shapes, sensitive to noise
Complete max⁡a,bd(a,b)\max_{a,b}d(a,b)maxa,b​d(a,b) Prefers compact clusters of similar diameter; sensitive to outliers
Average (UPGMA) mean of d(a,b)d(a,b)d(a,b) Compromise between the two
Ward Merge that minimizes the increase in total within-cluster SSE Behaves like a hierarchical k-means; tends to equal-sized compact clusters; defined for Euclidean distance

Cost: a naive implementation needs the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) distance matrix in memory and roughly O(n2log⁡n)O(n^2\log n)O(n2logn) to O(n3)O(n^3)O(n3) time depending on the algorithm, so it does not scale to very large nnn without approximations. Merges are greedy and irreversible: an early bad merge is never undone. The cophenetic correlation (correlation between original distances and the dendrogram's merge heights) measures how faithfully the tree represents the data. Hierarchical methods accept any dissimilarity, which makes them handy for non-Euclidean data (edit distance, correlation distance) when the matrix is small enough.

#DBSCAN

DBSCAN defines clusters as connected regions of high density. It has two parameters: a radius ε\varepsilonε and a count minPts.

  • A core point has at least minPts points (itself included) within ε\varepsilonε.
  • A border point lies within ε\varepsilonε of a core point but is not itself core.
  • Everything else is noise.

Clusters are formed by chaining core points that lie within ε\varepsilonε of one another, plus their border points. Strengths: no kkk needed, arbitrary cluster shapes, an explicit noise label, and good behavior with outliers. Weaknesses:

  • A single global ε\varepsilonε cannot fit clusters of very different density: a value that separates dense clusters will label a sparse cluster as noise, while one that keeps the sparse cluster together merges the dense ones.
  • High dimensions. Distance concentration makes ε\varepsilonε hard to set; run it after dimensionality reduction.
  • Parameter sensitivity. A kkk-distance plot (sorted distance to the minPts\text{minPts}minPts-th neighbor) helps choose ε\varepsilonε at its knee. Heuristics: minPts at least d+1d+1d+1, often about 2d2d2d, larger for noisy data.
  • Border points can be assigned order-dependently when reachable from two clusters.
  • With a spatial index in low dimensions it is roughly O(nlog⁡n)O(n\log n)O(nlogn); the worst case is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2).

#HDBSCAN

HDBSCAN removes the global ε\varepsilonε. It transforms distances into a mutual reachability distance (inflating distances in sparse regions using each point's core distance), builds a minimum spanning tree and the resulting single-linkage hierarchy, condenses the tree by discarding splits that shed fewer than min_cluster_size points, and then extracts the clusters that persist longest (maximum stability). It can therefore find clusters of varying density, labels low-density points as noise, and is much less sensitive to parameters, with min_cluster_size being the main one. It is generally the preferred density method when density varies, at higher compute cost than k-means; it still struggles in raw high-dimensional space.

Interview angle

"Your clusters are crescents and there are outliers. What now?" Answer: k-means will fail (convex Voronoi cells, outlier-sensitive). Use DBSCAN or HDBSCAN, after scaling and possibly PCA; use HDBSCAN if densities differ; check the noise fraction; and validate with stability or with a task-specific check rather than silhouette, which assumes convexity.

#Evaluating clusters without ground truth

If labels exist for a held-out subset, external indices apply: the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI, chance-corrected agreement between two partitions) and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI). Treat them as sanity checks: labels may encode a different notion of grouping than the data's dominant structure. Without labels, use internal indices and stability.

Silhouette. For point iii let a(i)a(i)a(i) be its mean distance to the other points in its own cluster and b(i)b(i)b(i) the smallest mean distance to the points of any other cluster. Then

s(i)=b(i)−a(i)max⁡{a(i),b(i)}∈[−1,1].s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),b(i)\}}\in[-1,1].s(i)=max{a(i),b(i)}b(i)−a(i)​∈[−1,1].

Values near 111 mean well inside the cluster; near 000 means on a boundary; negative suggests a likely mislabel. Average silhouette across points scores the whole partition. Cost is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) unless subsampled.

Stability assessment. Draw many bootstrap or subsample resamples, cluster each, and compare partitions on shared points (for example with ARI). High agreement suggests the structure is a property of the data rather than of the sample or initialization. Also compare across algorithms and random seeds.

Internal indices are not truth

Internal indices measure whether a clustering matches the geometric assumption that index encodes. A method that optimizes the same geometry (k-means and silhouette) will score well by construction. Say so, and add a task-based check: do the segments differ on a metric you care about, are they interpretable, are they stable over time?

#Dimensionality reduction for visualization: t-SNE and UMAP

PCA (linear, global variance; see Eigenvectors, PCA and SVD) is the first tool for compression and denoising. For visualizing nonlinear structure in 2D or 3D, neighbor-embedding methods are the usual choice. Both build a neighborhood graph in the original space and find low-dimensional coordinates that reproduce it.

#t-SNE

  1. In the original space, for each point iii define a Gaussian neighbor distribution pj∣i∝exp⁡(−∥xi−xj∥2/2σi2)p_{j\mid i}\propto\exp(-\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)pj∣i​∝exp(−∥xi​−xj​∥2/2σi2​), with σi\sigma_iσi​ found by binary search so that the entropy of PiP_iPi​ matches the chosen perplexity, Perp=2H(Pi)\text{Perp}=2^{H(P_i)}Perp=2H(Pi​). Perplexity acts as a soft "effective number of neighbors". Symmetrize: pij=(pj∣i+pi∣j)/2np_{ij}=(p_{j\mid i}+p_{i\mid j})/2npij​=(pj∣i​+pi∣j​)/2n.
  2. In the embedding, use a Student-ttt kernel with one degree of freedom (heavy tails): qij∝(1+∥yi−yj∥2)−1q_{ij}\propto(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}qij​∝(1+∥yi​−yj​∥2)−1, normalized over all pairs.
  3. Minimize KL(P∥Q)=∑i≠jpijlog⁡pijqij\mathrm{KL}(P\Vert Q)=\sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}KL(P∥Q)=∑i=j​pij​logqij​pij​​ over the yiy_iyi​ by gradient descent.

Why this design: KL(P∥Q)\mathrm{KL}(P\Vert Q)KL(P∥Q) is asymmetric. Placing two near points far apart (large ppp, small qqq) is penalized heavily, while placing two far points near each other (small ppp, large qqq) costs little. So local neighborhoods are preserved well; global arrangement is not guaranteed. The heavy-tailed kernel lets moderately dissimilar points sit farther apart in 2D than a Gaussian would, relieving the "crowding problem" (in 2D there is not enough room for all the moderately distant neighbors that exist in high dimensions).

Practical facts: the objective is non-convex, so runs differ by initialization and seed; results depend on perplexity (commonly 5 to 50, smaller than nnn; try several) and on enough iterations and an adequate learning rate; the Barnes-Hut and FFT-based approximations give about O(nlog⁡n)O(n\log n)O(nlogn) or better; and classic t-SNE has no out-of-sample mapping for new points.

#UMAP

UMAP builds a weighted (kkk-nearest-neighbor) graph in the original space, interpreted as a fuzzy topological representation, then optimizes an embedding whose fuzzy graph matches it by minimizing a cross-entropy between edge weights, using attractive forces along edges and repulsive forces from sampled non-edges. Its two main hyperparameters are n_neighbors (local versus global emphasis: larger values capture broader structure) and min_dist (how tightly points may pack in the embedding; smaller values give tighter clumps). Compared with t-SNE, UMAP is typically faster, can embed new points via a fitted transform, supports supervised and other-metric variants, and often (not always) retains more of the large-scale layout, partly owing to its spectral initialization rather than purely the objective.

#What you may and may not read from the plot

Read with care Reason
Distances between clusters Not preserved; far apart on the plot does not mean far apart in the data
Cluster sizes and densities t-SNE's per-point bandwidth σi\sigma_iσi​ equalizes local density; UMAP's min_dist rescales it, so a tight blob can be a diffuse one

Good practice: run several seeds and hyperparameter settings; confirm clusters with a method on the original (or PCA-reduced) space; and do not feed raw 2D t-SNE coordinates to a downstream model as if they were faithful features. A common pipeline is PCA to roughly 30 to 100 dimensions to denoise and speed up, then t-SNE or UMAP.

Interview angle

"Two clusters look far apart in the t-SNE plot; are they more different than two that look close?" Answer: not necessarily; t-SNE preserves local neighborhoods, not global distances or cluster sizes. Use it to generate hypotheses, then verify in the original space.

#Autoencoders (briefly)

An autoencoder learns an encoder z=fϕ(x)z=f_\phi(x)z=fϕ​(x) to a low-dimensional code and a decoder x^=gθ(z)\hat x=g_\theta(z)x^=gθ​(z), minimizing a reconstruction loss such as ∥x−x^∥2\lVert x-\hat x\rVert^2∥x−x^∥2. The bottleneck forces compression. A single-hidden-layer linear autoencoder with squared error recovers the same subspace as PCA (though not necessarily the individual principal axes); nonlinear activations and depth allow curved manifolds. Variants: denoising (reconstruct clean xxx from a corrupted input, discouraging the identity map), sparse (penalty on activations), contractive, and variational (VAE: a probabilistic latent space regularized toward a prior, enabling sampling). Uses: learned embeddings for downstream models, denoising, and reconstruction-based anomaly detection (Anomaly Detection). Cautions: an over-capacity network can learn near-identity and reconstruct everything; the code is not guaranteed to be interpretable or to preserve distances; and results depend on architecture and training stability.
